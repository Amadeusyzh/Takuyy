Why this matters

Embodied AI is the part of AI that has to move a body through the physical world. Modern robot learning papers talk about “policies”, “action chunks” and “end-effector deltas”, but underneath every one of them sits a stack of classical robotics: coordinate frames, kinematics, dynamics, low-level controllers and planners. A Vision-Language-Action model that outputs a 7-D end-effector command is useless unless something converts that command into joint torques at 1 kHz without hitting joint limits or itself.

This first post of the Embodied AI track is a compact tour of that stack, written for readers who know deep learning, graphics and world models but have little robotics background. If you have done graphics you already know half of it: a robot arm is a scene graph of rigid transforms, and inverse kinematics is the same problem that animators solve for character rigs.

Prerequisites

  • Linear algebra: matrices, rotations, least squares, the SVD.
  • Calculus: partial derivatives, the chain rule.
  • Python and NumPy.
  • Helpful but optional: homogeneous coordinates from a graphics course.

1. Rigid-body transforms and SE(3)

Rotations: SO(3)

The orientation of a rigid body relative to a reference frame is a rotation matrix $R \in \mathbb{R}^{3\times3}$ whose columns are the body’s axes expressed in the reference frame. Rotation matrices form the special orthogonal group

\[SO(3) = \{ R \in \mathbb{R}^{3\times 3} \;:\; R^\top R = I,\; \det R = 1 \}.\]

SO(3) is a 3-dimensional manifold, not a vector space: you cannot add two rotations and get a rotation. Common parameterizations are Euler angles (minimal but singular at “gimbal lock”), unit quaternions (4 numbers, double cover, no singularities, the standard in simulators and game engines), and the axis-angle / exponential coordinates $\omega\theta$ with unit axis $\hat\omega$ and angle $\theta$.

For a vector $\omega = (\omega_1, \omega_2, \omega_3)$ define the skew-symmetric matrix

\[[\omega] = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -\omega_3 & \omega_2 \\ \omega_3 & 0 & -\omega_1 \\ -\omega_2 & \omega_1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad [\omega]\,v = \omega \times v .\]

The matrix exponential maps axis-angle to a rotation (Rodrigues’ formula):

\[R = e^{[\hat\omega]\theta} = I + \sin\theta\,[\hat\omega] + (1-\cos\theta)\,[\hat\omega]^2 .\]

The set of skew-symmetric matrices is the Lie algebra $\mathfrak{so}(3)$, the tangent space of SO(3) at the identity. Angular velocity lives there: if $R(t)$ is a trajectory, then $\dot R R^\top = [\omega_s]$ gives the angular velocity in the fixed (space) frame and $R^\top \dot R = [\omega_b]$ in the body frame.

The figure shows Rodrigues’ formula at work: the component of $v$ along the axis stays fixed, and the rest of $v$ sweeps a circle around the axis by the angle $\theta$.

Rigid motions: SE(3)

A full pose (position $p$ and orientation $R$) is a homogeneous transform

\[T = \begin{bmatrix} R & p \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \in SE(3), \qquad T^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} R^\top & -R^\top p \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}.\]

This is exactly the 4x4 model matrix from graphics. Notation that saves a lot of bugs: write $T_{ab}$ for “the pose of frame $b$ expressed in frame $a$”. Then composition chains like dominoes,

\[T_{ac} = T_{ab}\,T_{bc}, \qquad p_a = T_{ab}\, p_b \;\text{(in homogeneous coordinates)}.\]

The robot’s whole “scene graph” (world, base, links, camera, gripper, object) is a tree of such transforms. Hand-eye calibration, for example, is the problem of estimating the fixed $T_{\text{gripper},\text{camera}}$.

Twists and screws

The velocity of a rigid body is a twist $\mathcal{V} = (\omega, v) \in \mathbb{R}^6$, with $[\mathcal{V}] = \begin{bmatrix}[\omega] & v\0&0\end{bmatrix} \in \mathfrak{se}(3)$. Chasles’ theorem says every rigid motion is a rotation about an axis combined with a translation along it, a screw motion, and the exponential map $T = e^{[\mathcal{S}]\theta}$ turns a unit screw axis $\mathcal{S}$ and a distance $\theta$ into an element of SE(3). This is the foundation of the product-of-exponentials (PoE) formula used throughout Modern Robotics. The dual of a twist is a wrench $\mathcal{F} = (m, f)$ (moment and force); power is $\mathcal{F}^\top\mathcal{V}$.

Why care as a learning person? Because action spaces are poses. Predicting a rotation with a network means choosing a representation: Euler angles have discontinuities, quaternions have the $q \equiv -q$ ambiguity, and Zhou et al. (CVPR 2019) showed that a continuous 6-D representation (two columns of $R$, re-orthonormalized) is easier to regress. Many policies (e.g. Diffusion Policy in post 4) use it.

Chasles’ theorem is easy to see in a picture: applying $e^{[\mathcal{S}]\theta}$ for growing $\theta$ moves a frame along a helix around the screw axis.

2. Forward kinematics

A serial manipulator is a chain of links connected by joints, usually revolute (R) or prismatic (P). The configuration is the vector of joint values $q \in \mathbb{R}^n$; the set of all configurations is the configuration space (C-space). Forward kinematics (FK) maps $q$ to the end-effector pose:

\[T_{0n}(q) = T_{01}(q_1)\,T_{12}(q_2)\cdots T_{n-1,n}(q_n).\]

Denavit-Hartenberg parameters

Denavit and Hartenberg (1955) showed that by placing frames cleverly, each link transform needs only four numbers. In the standard DH convention the $z_i$ axis is the axis of joint $i+1$ and

\[T_{i-1,i} = \mathrm{Rot}_z(\theta_i)\,\mathrm{Trans}_z(d_i)\,\mathrm{Trans}_x(a_i)\,\mathrm{Rot}_x(\alpha_i) = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta_i & -\sin\theta_i\cos\alpha_i & \sin\theta_i\sin\alpha_i & a_i\cos\theta_i\\ \sin\theta_i & \cos\theta_i\cos\alpha_i & -\cos\theta_i\sin\alpha_i & a_i\sin\theta_i\\ 0 & \sin\alpha_i & \cos\alpha_i & d_i\\ 0&0&0&1 \end{bmatrix}.\]
Parameter Meaning
$a_i$ link length: distance between $z_{i-1}$ and $z_i$ along $x_i$
$\alpha_i$ link twist: angle from $z_{i-1}$ to $z_i$ about $x_i$
$d_i$ link offset: distance along $z_{i-1}$ (variable for a prismatic joint)
$\theta_i$ joint angle about $z_{i-1}$ (variable for a revolute joint)

Beware: there is also a modified DH convention (Craig), used e.g. in Franka Emika’s documentation, with a different order of the four elementary transforms. Mixing the two is a classic source of bugs. In practice robots are described with URDF or MJCF files (post 3) that store a full transform per joint, and libraries such as Pinocchio, Drake, MuJoCo or PyBullet compute FK for you. DH is still worth knowing because it makes the structure explicit and is how most textbooks and datasheets present kinematics.

The figure places the four parameters on two consecutive joint axes. Read the transform from left to right: rotate by $\theta_i$ about $z_{i-1}$, slide $d_i$ along it, move $a_i$ along the new $x_i$, then tilt by $\alpha_i$ about $x_i$.

A planar 3-link arm in NumPy

For a planar arm, every $\alpha_i = d_i = 0$, so each DH transform is a rotation by $q_i$ followed by a translation $a_i$ along the new $x$ axis. With absolute angles $c_i = q_1 + \dots + q_i$, the end effector is

\[p_x = \sum_i a_i\cos c_i,\qquad p_y = \sum_i a_i \sin c_i,\qquad \phi = c_n .\] 
import numpy as np

def dh(a, alpha, d, theta):
    """Standard DH transform: Rot_z(theta) Trans_z(d) Trans_x(a) Rot_x(alpha)."""
    ct, st, ca, sa = np.cos(theta), np.sin(theta), np.cos(alpha), np.sin(alpha)
    return np.array([[ct, -st * ca,  st * sa, a * ct],
                     [st,  ct * ca, -ct * sa, a * st],
                     [0.0,     sa,       ca,      d],
                     [0.0,    0.0,      0.0,    1.0]])

LINKS = np.array([1.0, 0.8, 0.5])          # link lengths of a planar 3R arm

def fk(q, links=LINKS):
    """Forward kinematics: returns the 4x4 pose of the end effector and all joint positions."""
    T = np.eye(4)
    pts = [T[:3, 3].copy()]
    for a, theta in zip(links, q):
        T = T @ dh(a, 0.0, 0.0, theta)     # planar arm: alpha = d = 0
        pts.append(T[:3, 3].copy())
    return T, np.array(pts)

def task(q):
    """Task-space coordinates x = (px, py, phi) of the end effector."""
    T, _ = fk(q)
    return np.array([T[0, 3], T[1, 3], np.arctan2(T[1, 0], T[0, 0])])

pts gives every joint position, which is all you need to draw the arm with matplotlib.

3. Jacobians and differential kinematics

FK is nonlinear, but its derivative is linear in the joint velocities:

\[\dot x = J(q)\,\dot q, \qquad J(q) = \frac{\partial x}{\partial q} \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times n}.\]

For our planar arm ($m = n = 3$) the columns are easy to derive by differentiating the sums above:

\[J_{1i} = -\sum_{k\ge i} a_k \sin c_k,\qquad J_{2i} = \sum_{k\ge i} a_k\cos c_k, \qquad J_{3i} = 1 .\]

For a general spatial arm, the geometric Jacobian column of a revolute joint $i$ with unit axis $z_{i-1}$ through point $o_{i-1}$ (both in the base frame) is

\[J_i = \begin{bmatrix} z_{i-1}\times(o_n - o_{i-1}) \\ z_{i-1}\end{bmatrix},\]

stacking linear velocity over angular velocity; a prismatic joint contributes $(z_{i-1}, 0)$. (Modern Robotics writes the same thing with space and body Jacobians built from screw axes.)

The Jacobian tells you almost everything about local behaviour:

  • Singularities: where $\operatorname{rank} J < m$ the end effector loses a direction of motion (a fully stretched arm cannot move further outward). Near them, small Cartesian motions require huge joint speeds.
  • Manipulability: Yoshikawa’s measure $w = \sqrt{\det(JJ^\top)}$, the volume of the velocity ellipsoid $\lbrace J\dot q : \lVert\dot q\rVert \le 1 \rbrace$, whose axes come from the SVD of $J$.
  • Statics: by the principle of virtual work, a wrench $\mathcal{F}$ at the end effector requires joint torques $\tau = J^\top(q)\,\mathcal{F}$. This one line is the basis of force and impedance control.
  • Redundancy: if $n > m$ (e.g. a 7-DoF arm for a 6-D pose) there is a null space; joint motions $(I - J^+J)\,z$ move the elbow without moving the hand.

Always test a hand-written Jacobian against finite differences:

def jacobian(q, links=LINKS):
    """Analytic Jacobian d(px, py, phi)/dq of the planar arm."""
    c = np.cumsum(q)                        # absolute link angles
    J = np.zeros((3, len(q)))
    for i in range(len(q)):
        J[0, i] = -np.sum(links[i:] * np.sin(c[i:]))
        J[1, i] = np.sum(links[i:] * np.cos(c[i:]))
        J[2, i] = 1.0
    return J

q = np.array([0.3, -0.5, 0.8])
eps = 1e-6
J_num = np.stack([(task(q + eps * np.eye(3)[i]) - task(q - eps * np.eye(3)[i])) / (2 * eps)
                  for i in range(3)], axis=1)
print("Jacobian error:", np.abs(J_num - jacobian(q)).max())   # ~1e-10

4. Inverse kinematics

Inverse kinematics (IK) asks for $q$ such that \(x(q) = x^{\ast}\). It can have zero, finitely many, or infinitely many solutions.

Analytic IK

For simple or specially designed arms there are closed-form solutions. For a planar 2-link arm reaching $(p_x, p_y)$, the law of cosines gives

\[\cos q_2 = \frac{p_x^2 + p_y^2 - a_1^2 - a_2^2}{2a_1a_2},\qquad q_2 = \pm\arccos(\cdot),\qquad q_1 = \operatorname{atan2}(p_y, p_x) - \operatorname{atan2}(a_2\sin q_2,\; a_1 + a_2\cos q_2),\]

with the $\pm$ selecting “elbow up” or “elbow down”, and no solution when $\lvert\cos q_2\rvert > 1$. Industrial 6-DoF arms with a spherical wrist (last three axes intersecting) decouple into a position problem and an orientation problem (Pieper’s criterion) and have up to 8 analytic solutions. Tools such as IKFast (OpenRAVE) generate these solvers automatically.

Numerical IK: Newton, Jacobian transpose, pseudoinverse

For general arms we iterate. Linearize around the current $q$: \(x^{\ast} - x(q) \approx J\,\Delta q\). Options:

  • Jacobian transpose: $\Delta q = \alpha J^\top e$. Cheap, always a descent direction for $\tfrac12\lVert e\rVert^2$, but slow.
  • Pseudoinverse (Gauss-Newton): $\Delta q = J^+ e$ with $J^+ = J^\top(JJ^\top)^{-1}$ for a wide $J$. Fast near a solution but explodes near singularities because $J^+$ has $1/\sigma_i$ terms.
  • Damped least squares (DLS, Levenberg-Marquardt): minimize $\lVert J\Delta q - e\rVert^2 + \lambda^2\lVert\Delta q\rVert^2$, giving
\[\Delta q = J^\top\left(JJ^\top + \lambda^2 I\right)^{-1} e = \sum_i \frac{\sigma_i}{\sigma_i^2 + \lambda^2}\, v_i u_i^\top e .\]

The SVD form shows why DLS is robust (Nakamura and Hanafusa 1986; Wampler 1986): directions with $\sigma_i \gg \lambda$ behave like the pseudoinverse, while directions with $\sigma_i \to 0$ are damped to zero instead of blowing up. The price is slower convergence and a small bias.

def wrap(a):
    return (a + np.pi) % (2 * np.pi) - np.pi

def ik_dls(x_goal, q0, lam=0.05, iters=200, tol=1e-6):
    """Damped least-squares IK: dq = J^T (J J^T + lam^2 I)^-1 e."""
    q = q0.astype(float).copy()
    for k in range(iters):
        e = x_goal - task(q)
        e[2] = wrap(e[2])                   # angle error must be wrapped
        if np.linalg.norm(e) < tol:
            break
        J = jacobian(q)
        dq = J.T @ np.linalg.solve(J @ J.T + lam**2 * np.eye(3), e)
        q += dq
    return q, k, np.linalg.norm(e)

x_goal = np.array([1.2, 0.9, np.pi / 4])
q_sol, n, err = ik_dls(x_goal, np.zeros(3))
print(np.round(q_sol, 4), n, err)           # converges in ~8 iterations, residual ~1e-7
print(np.round(task(q_sol), 4))             # [1.2, 0.9, 0.7854]

# unreachable target (total reach is 2.3): with lam=0.5 the arm stretches toward it,
# residual 0.7 = the best achievable. With lam=0.05 the iteration oscillates near the singularity.
q_far, n, err = ik_dls(np.array([3.0, 0.0, 0.0]), np.array([0.1, 0.1, 0.1]), lam=0.5, iters=500)
print(err)                                  # 0.7

Try the last call with lam=0.05: the iterate wanders around with a residual of several units, because the stretched-out arm is singular and lightly-damped steps overshoot. Production IK solvers combine DLS with adaptive damping, step-size limits, joint limits and secondary objectives in the null space; the modern formulation is a small quadratic program (QP) per control tick, e.g. “minimize task error subject to joint position and velocity limits”, solved by libraries such as Pink, mink or Drake.

5. A short look at dynamics

Kinematics ignores forces. The equations of motion of an $n$-joint rigid-body system are

\[M(q)\,\ddot q + C(q,\dot q)\,\dot q + g(q) = \tau + J(q)^\top \mathcal{F}_{\text{ext}},\]

with the symmetric positive-definite mass matrix $M(q)$, Coriolis and centrifugal terms $C\dot q$, gravity $g(q)$, joint torques $\tau$ and external wrenches \(\mathcal{F}_{\text{ext}}\) (e.g. contacts). They come from the Euler-Lagrange equations with Lagrangian $L = \tfrac12 \dot q^\top M(q)\dot q - V(q)$. Two standard problems:

  • Inverse dynamics: given $q, \dot q, \ddot q$, compute $\tau$. The recursive Newton-Euler algorithm (RNEA) does this in $O(n)$.
  • Forward dynamics: given $\tau$, compute $\ddot q = M^{-1}(\tau - C\dot q - g)$. The articulated-body algorithm (ABA) is also $O(n)$ (Featherstone 2008).

Two structural facts make control design possible: $M(q)$ is positive definite, and with a suitable choice of $C$ the matrix $\dot M - 2C$ is skew-symmetric (passivity). Contacts, friction and actuator limits make real dynamics much harder; that is the job of the physics engines in post 3.

6. Control

The controller turns a desired trajectory into torques, typically at 0.5 to 1 kHz on the robot, while a learned policy runs at 5 to 50 Hz on top of it. Knowing which layer your policy commands (joint position targets, joint velocities, end-effector poses, torques) matters a lot for sim-to-real.

PID and PD with gravity compensation

The workhorse is PID on each joint:

\[\tau = K_p\, e + K_i \int_0^t e\, dt' - K_d\,\dot q, \qquad e = q_{\text{ref}} - q .\]

The proportional term acts as a spring, the derivative term as a damper, and the integral term removes steady-state error caused by constant disturbances such as gravity. A better use of a model is PD plus gravity compensation, $\tau = K_p e - K_d\dot q + g(q)$, which for a constant target is globally asymptotically stable (Takegaki and Arimoto 1981). A one-joint pendulum makes the difference visible:

import numpy as np

# 1-DoF pendulum joint: I*qdd + b*qd + m*g*l*sin(q) = tau
I, b, m, g, l = 0.5, 0.1, 1.0, 9.81, 0.5
dt, T = 1e-3, 3.0

def simulate(Kp, Kd, Ki, gravity_comp, q_ref=np.pi / 3):
    q, qd, integ = 0.0, 0.0, 0.0
    for _ in range(int(T / dt)):
        e = q_ref - q
        integ += e * dt
        tau = Kp * e - Kd * qd + Ki * integ
        if gravity_comp:
            tau += m * g * l * np.sin(q)      # feed-forward model term
        qdd = (tau - b * qd - m * g * l * np.sin(q)) / I
        qd += qdd * dt                        # semi-implicit Euler
        q += qd * dt
    return q_ref - q

print("PD only        error %.4f rad" % simulate(20, 3, 0, False))   # 0.1861
print("PID            error %.4f rad" % simulate(20, 3, 30, False))  # 0.0000
print("PD + gravity   error %.4f rad" % simulate(20, 3, 0, True))    # -0.0001

Plain PD sags by about 0.19 rad because the spring must hold up the weight; the integral term or the gravity model removes the error. Legged-robot RL policies (post 2) almost always output joint position targets to such a PD loop instead of raw torques; the PD gains then become part of the “action space”.

Computed torque and operational-space control

If the model is good, cancel the nonlinear dynamics and impose linear error dynamics (computed torque, or feedback linearization):

\[\tau = M(q)\left(\ddot q_{\text{ref}} + K_d\dot e + K_p e\right) + C\dot q + g .\]

Khatib’s operational-space formulation (1987) does the same in task space. With the task-space inertia $\Lambda(q) = (J M^{-1} J^\top)^{-1}$ the end effector behaves like a body of mass $\Lambda$, and

\[\tau = J^\top\left(\Lambda\,\ddot x_{\text{cmd}} + \mu + p\right) + \left(I - J^\top \bar J^{\top}\right)\tau_0, \qquad \bar J = M^{-1}J^\top\Lambda,\]

where $\mu, p$ are the task-space Coriolis and gravity terms and $\tau_0$ is a secondary objective projected into the dynamically consistent null space (e.g. keep the elbow near a rest pose).

Impedance control

Hogan’s impedance control (1985) does not try to track a position stiffly; it makes the end effector behave like a mass-spring-damper around a target:

\[\Lambda_d\,\ddot{\tilde x} + D_d\,\dot{\tilde x} + K_d\,\tilde x = \mathcal{F}_{\text{ext}},\qquad \tilde x = x - x_d .\]

The simplest implementation ignores the desired inertia and commands $\tau = J^\top\left(K_d (x_d - x) - D_d\,\dot x\right) + g(q)$, using the static relation $\tau = J^\top\mathcal{F}$. Low stiffness gives compliant, safe contact (wiping, insertion, human interaction); high stiffness gives accurate tracking. Many learned manipulation policies output a target pose $x_d$ at 10 to 20 Hz to a Cartesian impedance controller running at 1 kHz; some also predict the stiffness (“variable impedance”).

7. Motion planning

Planning finds a collision-free path in C-space from $q_{\text{start}}$ to $q_{\text{goal}}$. Obstacles in the workspace become complicated “C-obstacles” in joint space, so planners query a collision checker (which runs FK and mesh-mesh tests, the same BVH machinery as in ray tracing) instead of building the free space explicitly.

Graph search: A*

On a discretized C-space (grid or lattice), A* (Hart, Nilsson and Raphael 1968) expands nodes in order of $f(n) = g(n) + h(n)$, cost-so-far plus a heuristic estimate of cost-to-go. If $h$ is admissible (never overestimates) A* returns an optimal path; if it is also consistent (\(h(n) \le c(n,n') + h(n')\)) no node needs to be re-expanded. Dijkstra is A* with $h = 0$. Grids scale exponentially with dimension, so A* is used for mobile robots in 2-D/3-D, footstep planning and discretized lattices, not for 7-DoF arms.

Sampling-based planning: PRM, RRT, RRT*

Sampling-based planners avoid discretization. PRM (Kavraki et al. 1996) samples many collision-free configurations, connects nearby ones and answers queries with graph search. RRT (LaValle 1998) grows a tree from the start: sample $x_{\text{rand}}$, find the nearest tree node, step a fixed distance toward the sample, and add the new node if the edge is collision free. The Voronoi bias of this procedure pulls the tree into unexplored space. RRT is probabilistically complete (it finds a path with probability approaching 1 if one exists) but not optimal. RRT* (Karaman and Frazzoli 2011) adds two steps, choosing the best parent within a shrinking radius $r_n \propto (\log n / n)^{1/d}$ and rewiring neighbours through the new node, which makes it asymptotically optimal.

import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
OBST = [(np.array([0.5, 0.5]), 0.2), (np.array([0.25, 0.8]), 0.12)]   # circles (center, radius)

def free(p):
    return all(np.linalg.norm(p - c) > r for c, r in OBST)

def edge_free(a, b, n=20):
    return all(free(a + t * (b - a)) for t in np.linspace(0, 1, n))

def rrt(start, goal, step=0.05, iters=5000, goal_bias=0.1):
    nodes, parent = [start], [-1]
    for _ in range(iters):
        x_rand = goal if rng.random() < goal_bias else rng.random(2)   # sample in [0,1]^2
        i = int(np.argmin([np.linalg.norm(n - x_rand) for n in nodes]))
        d = x_rand - nodes[i]
        x_new = nodes[i] + d * min(1.0, step / (np.linalg.norm(d) + 1e-12))  # steer
        if not edge_free(nodes[i], x_new):
            continue
        nodes.append(x_new); parent.append(i)
        if np.linalg.norm(x_new - goal) < step and edge_free(x_new, goal):
            nodes.append(goal); parent.append(len(nodes) - 2)
            path, j = [], len(nodes) - 1
            while j != -1:
                path.append(nodes[j]); j = parent[j]
            return np.array(path[::-1]), len(nodes)
    return None, len(nodes)

path, n = rrt(np.array([0.1, 0.1]), np.array([0.9, 0.9]))
print(f"tree size {n}, path waypoints {len(path)}, "
      f"length {np.sum(np.linalg.norm(np.diff(path, axis=0), axis=1)):.3f}")

To plan for the 3-link arm instead of a point, replace the sample space by $[-\pi,\pi]^3$ and free(q) by “FK the links and test each segment against the obstacles”. RRT paths are jagged, so real systems shortcut and smooth them, then time-parameterize them under velocity and acceleration limits (e.g. TOPP-RA). Trajectory optimization methods such as CHOMP (Ratliff et al. 2009) and TrajOpt (Schulman et al. 2013) instead optimize a whole trajectory for smoothness plus collision cost, and GPU-parallel planners like NVIDIA cuRobo bring planning times to milliseconds.

Learning rarely removes the lower layers: an OpenVLA or pi0 policy still sends targets to an IK solver and an impedance or PD controller. Understanding those layers is how you debug a policy that “works in sim but jitters on the robot”.

Try it

The home page Lab has an XPBD cloth demo. Position-based dynamics solves constraints by projecting positions, much like the IK iterations above project an arm onto the “reach the target” constraint; dragging a cloth particle is literally an IK problem for a very redundant chain. A good exercise is to port ik_dls to JavaScript and drag the end effector of a 3-link arm on a canvas.

Exercises

  1. Implement the analytic 2-link IK above and check both elbow solutions with fk.
  2. Write the geometric Jacobian for a 3-D arm given by a DH table and verify it with finite differences. Plot the manipulability $\sqrt{\det JJ^\top}$ of the planar arm over its workspace.
  3. Add joint limits to ik_dls by clamping, then by a null-space term that pushes joints to the middle of their range. Compare how often each version reaches random targets.
  4. Extend the pendulum example to a 2-link arm with $M(q)$ and compare PD, PID and computed torque under a 10% error in the link masses.
  5. Turn rrt into RRT* (choose-parent and rewire) and plot path length versus number of samples.
