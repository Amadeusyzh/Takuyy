Why this matters

Post 1 introduced world models as small learned simulators trained per task, and post 2 built the generative toolkit. Between 2023 and 2025 the two lines merged: video generators trained on internet-scale data started to be described, and used, as world simulators: you give them a frame and a stream of actions, and they render what happens next. For a graphics person this is a striking shift: a learned model replaces the whole game engine (state update, physics and rendering) at once. This post surveys the key systems, explains the one genuinely new ingredient (latent actions learned from unlabeled video), and is honest about what these models still cannot do.

Prerequisites

  • Posts 1 and 2 (RSSM and imagination; tokenizers, autoregressive Transformers, latent diffusion, classifier-free guidance, DiT).
  • Familiarity with video tensors $(T, H, W, C)$ and with the idea of a game loop (input, state update, render).

1. From video prediction to world simulation

A video model learns $p_\theta(x_{1:T} \mid c)$ for clips $x_{1:T}$ and conditioning $c$ (text, a first frame). A world simulator additionally needs:

  1. Interactivity: a factorization $p_\theta(x_{t+1} \mid x_{\le t}, a_{\le t})$ that can be stepped frame by frame as actions arrive, not just a whole clip at once.
  2. Controllability: the actions must actually determine the outcome (a “move left” input moves the character left), and the space of actions must be meaningful.
  3. Consistency and memory: the world must stay the same when you look away and back, objects must persist, and the rollout must not drift into noise after a few hundred frames.
  4. Plausible dynamics: objects fall, collide and occlude as in the target domain (a game’s rules or real physics).
  5. Speed: for interaction it must run in real time, typically 20-30 frames per second.

2. Sora: “Video generation models as world simulators”

OpenAI’s February 2024 technical report, “Video generation models as world simulators”, describes Sora at a high level (it explicitly omits model and implementation details):

  • A video compression network maps raw video to a latent that is compressed in space and time (post 2, Section 6).
  • The latent is cut into spacetime patches, which act as tokens for a diffusion Transformer (post 2, Section 9). Because the model sees a variable-length set of patches, it can be trained on videos of native duration, resolution and aspect ratio instead of cropping everything to a fixed square.
  • Training videos are re-captioned with a descriptive captioner (the technique from DALL·E 3), and short user prompts are expanded by a language model.

Spacetime patches are easiest to see in 3D:

The report’s main claim is in its title: scaling video generation produces emergent simulation capabilities, which it lists as 3D consistency under camera motion, long-range coherence and object permanence (sometimes), simple interactions that change world state (a painter leaving strokes, a bite in a burger), and “simulating digital worlds” (it can render Minecraft while controlling the player with a basic policy). The same report lists failures: it does not accurately model the physics of many basic interactions such as glass shattering, and interactions like eating do not always yield correct state changes.

Two cautions are worth keeping in mind when you cite Sora as a world model. First, it is not action-conditioned: you cannot step it with control inputs, so it is a simulator only in a weak, “prompt it with a scene description” sense. Second, the evidence is qualitative (curated samples); post 5 discusses benchmarks that test such claims and find physical understanding to be limited.

3. Genie: generative interactive environments from unlabeled video

3.1 The problem: internet video has no actions

To train $p(x_{t+1} \mid x_{\le t}, a_t)$ you need action labels, but the vast majority of gameplay and real-world video on the internet comes without them. Genie (Bruce et al., “Genie: Generative Interactive Environments”, ICML 2024, arXiv:2402.15391) learns a controllable world model from about thirty thousand hours of 2D platformer gameplay videos without any action labels. Its 11B-parameter model has three parts:

  1. A spatiotemporal video tokenizer (a VQ-VAE with a memory-efficient ST-Transformer that alternates spatial and causal temporal attention), producing discrete tokens $z_t$ for every frame.
  2. A latent action model (LAM) that infers a discrete action $\tilde a_t$ between consecutive frames (Section 3.2).
  3. A dynamics model, a MaskGIT-style ST-Transformer that predicts the next frame’s tokens from past tokens and latent actions: $p(z_{t+1} \mid z_{\le t}, \tilde a_{\le t})$.

The figure shows how the three parts are wired during training and during play.

At inference time the LAM is discarded. A user (or an agent) picks one of the latent actions at every step, and the dynamics model plus tokenizer decoder render the result. Genie can be prompted with images it has never seen, including sketches and photographs, turning them into playable 2D worlds. The same recipe trained on robot videos learned consistent latent actions for a robot arm, and the paper shows that latent actions inferred from expert videos can be used to train a policy for an unseen game with very few labeled samples.

3.2 Latent action models

The LAM is an autoencoder with an information bottleneck in the middle. An encoder sees past frames $x_{1:t}$ and the next frame $x_{t+1}$ and outputs a continuous vector, which is quantized to one of a very small number of codes (Genie uses $\lvert \mathcal{A} \rvert = 8$). A decoder must reconstruct $x_{t+1}$ from the past frames and that code alone:

\[\tilde a_t = \mathrm{VQ}\big(\mathrm{Enc}_\phi(x_{1:t}, x_{t+1})\big) \in \lbrace e_1, \dots, e_{\lvert\mathcal{A}\rvert} \rbrace,\qquad \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{LAM}} = \big\lVert x_{t+1} - \mathrm{Dec}_\psi(x_{1:t}, \tilde a_t) \big\rVert^2 + \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{VQ}} .\]

Because the past frames are given to the decoder, the cheapest way to use the 3-bit bottleneck is to encode what changed and is not predictable from the past, which in a game is mostly the player’s input. The same principle appears in LAPO (Schmidt & Jiang, “Learning to Act without Actions”, ICLR 2024, arXiv:2312.10812), which recovers latent-action policies and world models from Procgen videos and maps latent actions to real ones with a tiny labeled set. An older alternative is VPT (Baker et al., NeurIPS 2022, arXiv:2206.11795): train an inverse dynamics model on a small amount of labeled Minecraft play and use it to pseudo-label 70k hours of internet video.

Latent actions have known weaknesses: they can capture changes not caused by the agent (camera shake, other characters), they are not aligned across domains, and a small discrete codebook cannot express continuous control well. Section 8 has a runnable toy LAM.

3.3 Genie 2 and Genie 3

Genie 2 (Google DeepMind blog, December 2024) moved to 3D. It is described as an autoregressive latent diffusion model: frames pass through an autoencoder, and a large Transformer dynamics model, trained with a causal mask like an LLM, generates the next latent frame by diffusion given past latents and keyboard/mouse actions. Classifier-free guidance is used to improve action controllability. Prompted with a single image (for example from Imagen 3), it produces worlds that stay consistent for up to a minute, remembers parts of the scene that leave the view, and models effects such as water, smoke, gravity and lighting. DeepMind used it to generate evaluation environments for its SIMA agent.

Genie 3 (Google DeepMind blog, August 2025) is generated from a text prompt and is the first in the series that is interactive in real time: the announcement states 24 frames per second at 720p with consistency over a few minutes. It also introduced promptable world events: text instructions that change the running world (weather, new objects or characters). The announcement lists limitations, including a limited action space for the agent, difficulties with multi-agent interactions, inaccurate rendering of real-world locations and text, and a limited interaction duration. No paper or architecture details were published at the time of the announcement, so treat specific capability claims as the company’s own.

Update (November 2026): Genie 3 became publicly usable as Project Genie in January 2026, and Genie 4 was announced as a research preview on 27 October 2026, adding native audio and language steering. See the 2025-2026 update section near the end of this post.

4. GameNGen: a diffusion model as a game engine

GameNGen (Valevski et al., “Diffusion Models Are Real-Time Game Engines”, ICLR 2025, arXiv:2408.14837) asks a narrow but sharp question: can a neural network be the game engine of a complex game? It simulates DOOM interactively at 20 frames per second on a single TPU, over multi-minute sessions. The recipe has two phases:

  1. Data: an RL agent learns to play DOOM, and all its training episodes (including the bad early ones, which give diverse coverage) are recorded as (frame, action) sequences.
  2. Model: Stable Diffusion v1.4 is re-purposed as a next-frame predictor. The text conditioning is replaced by embeddings of the last actions, and the past frames are encoded by the autoencoder and concatenated to the noisy latent along the channel axis:
\[p_\theta\big(x_{t+1} \mid x_{t-N+1:t},\ a_{t-N+1:t}\big)\]

Here $N$ is the number of past frames and actions in the context (64 in the paper).

The decisive trick is noise augmentation of the context. During training, the conditioning frames are corrupted with Gaussian noise of a random level, and that level is given to the model as an input. The model therefore learns to correct errors in its own context, which stops the autoregressive drift that otherwise destroys rollouts within a few seconds. The authors also fine-tune the latent decoder to reduce artifacts in the HUD, sample with only 4 DDIM steps, and report next-frame PSNR of 29.4 (comparable to lossy JPEG); human raters were only slightly better than chance at telling short clips of the real game from the simulation.

5. Action-conditioned real-world simulators

5.1 UniSim

UniSim (Yang et al., “Learning Interactive Real-World Simulators”, ICLR 2024 Outstanding Paper, arXiv:2310.06114) aims at the real world. Its insight is that different datasets carry different kinds of “actions”: text in image-caption data, motor commands in robot data, camera motion in navigation data, language descriptions of human activities. UniSim casts all of them as conditioning for one video diffusion model that predicts the next video segment given the history and the action. The paper then trains (a) a high-level vision-language planner and (b) a low-level RL policy purely inside the simulator and shows zero-shot transfer to real robots. It is the clearest demonstration that a video world model can be used as a simulator for training, not just for producing pretty videos.

5.2 GAIA-1 for driving

GAIA-1 (Hu et al., Wayve, arXiv:2309.17080, 2023) is a generative world model for autonomous driving. Video, text and ego-vehicle actions are encoded into tokens, an autoregressive Transformer world model predicts the next image tokens (future prediction as next-token prediction), and a separate video diffusion decoder renders high-resolution frames. Conditioning on actions lets one ask counterfactual questions (“what if the car steered left?”), and text prompts can change weather or time of day. Driving is a natural application because rare, dangerous scenarios are exactly what a simulator should generate.

Update (November 2026): the line continued with GAIA-2 (2025, multi-camera latent diffusion) and GAIA-4 (2026, closed-loop camera and radar simulation). Waymo built its own driving world model on Genie 3. See the update section.

5.3 NVIDIA Cosmos

Cosmos (NVIDIA, “Cosmos World Foundation Model Platform for Physical AI”, arXiv:2501.03575, 2025) frames world models as foundation models for physical AI. The platform includes a video curation pipeline, continuous and discrete Cosmos tokenizers, pre-trained world foundation models in both diffusion and autoregressive flavors, guardrails, and post-training examples that specialize the general model to camera control, robotic manipulation and autonomous driving. The models are released under the NVIDIA Open Model License (github.com/NVIDIA/Cosmos). The intended workflow is “pre-train once on broad video, post-train on your robot or car”, which mirrors how LLMs are adapted. Cosmos is a good starting point if you want to experiment with a large open video world model.

Update (November 2026): NVIDIA followed with Cosmos-Predict2.5 and Transfer2.5 (October 2025) and Cosmos 3 (June 2026), a mixture-of-transformers model that also reasons and outputs actions. See the update section.

6. Making autoregressive video rollouts stable

All interactive models generate frame by frame (or chunk by chunk) and condition on their own outputs, so they face the compounding-error problem from post 1 in a severe form. Techniques you will meet repeatedly:

  • Context noise augmentation (GameNGen, Genie 2-style models): train with corrupted context and tell the model the corruption level.
  • Diffusion Forcing (Chen et al., NeurIPS 2024, arXiv:2407.01392): give every frame in a sequence its own independent noise level during training. A causal model trained this way can denoise the next frames while past frames are clean or only slightly noisy, keeps the future uncertain and stabilizes long rollouts; it also enables guidance over whole future trajectories for planning.
  • Causal attention with KV caching so that each new frame costs a constant amount of compute, and step distillation (few-step or one-step samplers) to reach real-time rates.
  • Memory mechanisms: longer context windows, compressed memories of past frames, retrieval of frames by camera pose, or an explicit 3D representation of the scene. The last option leads directly to post 4.

The schemes differ only in which noise levels the frames of a training sequence get, as the figure shows.

7. What is still missing

  • Long-horizon consistency and memory. Turn around twice and the room changes. Pixel-space models have no persistent state other than their context window; consistency over minutes is a frontier claim, over hours unsolved. (Update, November 2026: open models with pose-indexed memory now report minute-scale consistency; hours and off-screen dynamics remain open. See the update section.)
  • Physics. Kang et al. (“How Far is Video Generation from World Model: A Physical Law Perspective”, ICML 2025, arXiv:2411.02385) trained video diffusion models on synthetic 2D physics and found near-perfect in-distribution generalization but failure out of distribution: the models behave like case-based retrieval (mimicking the closest training example) rather than learning laws. Physics-IQ (Motamed et al., WACV 2026, arXiv:2501.09038) found that physical understanding in current video models is severely limited and unrelated to visual realism. Post 5 returns to evaluation.
  • Controllability and action spaces. Keyboard actions in one game, latent actions in another, robot joint commands elsewhere: there is no shared action interface, which limits transfer.
  • Causality vs. correlation. A model trained on passive video may learn that “umbrellas cause rain”. Interventional data (actions) helps, but most video has none.
  • Compute. Real-time generation at high resolution needs heavy distillation and specialized hardware, and the training budgets are out of reach for most labs.
  • Evaluation. FVD and visual quality metrics say little about whether a simulator is useful for training agents (post 5).

8. Code: a toy latent action model

The script below makes the latent-action idea concrete. A dot moves on a $12 \times 12$ grid under five hidden actions (stay, up, down, left, right). A LAM with an 8-way discrete bottleneck is trained only on frame pairs $(x_t, x_{t+1})$; afterwards we check whether the learned codes align with the hidden actions, and we “play” the world model by feeding a chosen code repeatedly. For robustness at this tiny scale the bottleneck is a straight-through Gumbel-softmax rather than Genie’s VQ (both give a discrete code with gradients), and the encoder is convolutional with global pooling, i.e. translation invariant. It runs in about two minutes on a CPU.

import torch
import torch.nn as nn
import torch.nn.functional as F

torch.manual_seed(0)
G, K = 12, 8                                      # grid size, number of latent actions
MOVES = torch.tensor([[0, 0], [-1, 0], [1, 0], [0, -1], [0, 1]])   # stay, up, down, left, right

def render(pos):
    ys, xs = torch.meshgrid(torch.arange(G), torch.arange(G), indexing="ij")
    d2 = (ys[None] - pos[:, 0, None, None]) ** 2 + (xs[None] - pos[:, 1, None, None]) ** 2
    return torch.exp(-d2.float() / 0.5).reshape(len(pos), -1)

def sample_transitions(n):
    pos = torch.randint(1, G - 1, (n, 2))
    act = torch.randint(0, 5, (n,))
    nxt = (pos + MOVES[act]).clamp(0, G - 1)
    return render(pos), render(nxt), act

def mlp(i, o, h=256):
    return nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(i, h), nn.ReLU(), nn.Linear(h, h), nn.ReLU(), nn.Linear(h, o))

class ConvEncoder(nn.Module):
    """Translation-invariant: conv layers + global max pooling, so a code can only describe the motion."""
    def __init__(self):
        super().__init__()
        self.conv = nn.Sequential(nn.Conv2d(2, 32, 3, padding=1), nn.ReLU(), nn.Conv2d(32, 32, 3, padding=1), nn.ReLU())
        self.head = nn.Linear(32, K)

    def forward(self, x, x_next):
        h = self.conv(torch.stack([x, x_next], 1).view(-1, 2, G, G))
        return self.head(h.amax(dim=(2, 3)))

enc = ConvEncoder()
dec = mlp(G * G + K, G * G)
opt = torch.optim.Adam([*enc.parameters(), *dec.parameters()], lr=1e-3)
for step in range(3000):
    x, x_next, _ = sample_transitions(256)
    z = F.gumbel_softmax(enc(x, x_next), tau=1.0, hard=True)
    loss = F.mse_loss(dec(torch.cat([x, z], 1)), x_next) * 100
    opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); opt.step()
    if step % 1000 == 0:
        print(f"step {step}  loss {loss.item():.4f}")
with torch.no_grad():
    x, x_next, act = sample_transitions(5000)
    idx = enc(x, x_next).argmax(1)
    for a, name in enumerate(["stay", "up", "down", "left", "right"]):
        c = torch.bincount(idx[act == a], minlength=K)
        print(f"{name:>5}: code {c.argmax().item()}, purity {(c.max() / c.sum()).item():.2f}")
    right = torch.bincount(idx[act == 4], minlength=K).argmax()
    frame = render(torch.tensor([[6, 3]]))
    for t in range(4):
        frame = dec(torch.cat([frame, F.one_hot(right, K).float()[None]], 1))
        r, c = divmod(frame.argmax().item(), G)
        print(f"t={t + 1}: dot at row {r}, col {c}")

On this toy the five hidden actions typically map to distinct codes with high purity, and repeatedly pressing the “right” code moves the dot one cell to the right per step. Things to try: add a second, uncontrolled dot that moves randomly and see whether the codes start to encode its motion as well (a known failure mode of LAMs), or shrink the codebook to 4 and see which actions merge. Also replace ConvEncoder by an MLP on the concatenated frames: the reconstruction loss still goes down, but code purity drops to about 0.2-0.3 (close to chance), because the decoder also sees the current position and the meaning of each code can then change from one region of the grid to another. Latent actions are only consistent across states if the architecture or the training forces them to be, which is a real issue for LAMs trained on diverse video.

Google DeepMind: from Genie 3 to Project Genie and Genie 4

Section 3.3 described Genie 3 (August 2025): text-prompted worlds at 720p and 24 frames per second, consistent for a few minutes, with about one minute of visual memory. In January 2026 Google made it usable outside the lab: Project Genie (Google blog, 29 January 2026) is an experimental web app that combines Genie 3 with the Nano Banana Pro image model and Gemini. It was first offered to adult Google AI Ultra subscribers in the US, and generations are limited to 60 seconds. On 27 October 2026 DeepMind announced Genie 4 (DeepMind blog) as a research preview for trusted testers. The announcement highlights natively generated audio (ambient sound, music and dialogue that react to events), real-time steering with natural-language prompts, and better simulation of physics and human characters. Gemini models support the simulation: they adapt text prompts to the surrounding scene and keep the world progressing between inputs. The blog gives no resolution, frame rate or architecture, and it says coherence holds over short sessions and can drift over longer runs or after many edits. In the terms of section 7, the frontier claim is still “minutes, not hours”. Genie 3 also became the base of a domain model: the Waymo World Model (driving section below).

Open real-time interactive models

The most important 2025-2026 change for a learner is that you can now run interactive world models. Most open models share one recipe. They start from a large pretrained text- or image-to-video diffusion model (often Wan, see post 2). They make it causal and add action conditioning. Then they distill it into a few-step autoregressive sampler with a Self-Forcing-style objective (post 2’s update explains Self Forcing and its successors).

  • Matrix-Game 2.0 (He et al., preprint, arXiv:2508.13009, August 2025) is an open-source, real-time, streaming world model. It is trained on about 1,200 hours of interactive video with action annotations, produced by its own data pipeline in Unreal Engine and GTA5. It combines a causal few-step diffusion student with keyboard and mouse conditioning. It made the GameNGen idea (section 4) work for open-domain scenes and long streams. Matrix-Game 3.0 (Wang et al., preprint, arXiv:2604.08995, April 2026) adds memory: it retrieves past frames by camera pose, distills over several segments with DMD, and speeds up decoding with quantization and a pruned VAE decoder. It reports up to 40 fps at 720p for a 5B model and memory consistency over minute-long sequences.
  • Hunyuan-GameCraft (Li et al., preprint, arXiv:2506.17201, June 2025) maps keyboard and mouse inputs into a shared camera-motion space and mixes history conditions during training, so that long game-style videos keep reacting to inputs. Yan (Ye et al., preprint, arXiv:2508.08601, August 2025) is a framework of three parts: real-time simulation, multimodal generation, and editing of a running world with text.
  • WorldPlay (Sun et al., preprint, arXiv:2512.14614, December 2025) targets the speed-memory trade-off. It represents actions in two ways, rebuilds a compact context memory from past frames, and uses a “context forcing” distillation. It streams 720p at 24 fps with better long-term geometric consistency. AlayaWorld (preprint, arXiv:2607.18367, July 2026) is a 15B autoregressive video DiT at 24 fps. Its context is built from four parts: a “sink” frame, a compressed history, a spatial memory and the most recent frames. Distillation reduces each chunk from about 30 to four sampling steps.
  • LongLive (Yang et al., preprint, arXiv:2509.22622, September 2025) is not action-conditioned, but it shows the same machinery on long videos. A frame-level autoregressive model lets the user switch prompts during generation. The 1.3B model runs at 20.7 fps on one H100 and makes videos up to 240 s long.

Memory: the main research front

Section 7 named long-horizon consistency as the biggest gap, and most 2025-2026 papers attack it. The figure shows the basic budget problem. A model that keeps every past latent frame in context pays memory and attention cost that grows with the rollout length $T$. A sliding window of $W$ frames is cheap but forgets. The common compromise keeps the window and adds $K$ selected memory frames (or a compact 3D state), so the cost stays bounded while old places can still be recalled.

The memory designs fall into four families, roughly from implicit to explicit:

  1. Retrieval of past frames by camera pose. WorldMem (Xiao et al., preprint, arXiv:2504.12369) stores past frames with their poses and timestamps and attends to the relevant ones. Context as Memory (Yu et al., SIGGRAPH Asia 2025, arXiv:2506.03141) selects context frames whose camera frustum overlaps the current view. VMem (Li et al., ICCV 2025, arXiv:2506.18903) indexes past views by surfels. ReWorld (Chen et al., preprint, arXiv:2608.23565, August 2026) keeps a bounded KV cache with a pose-indexed landmark bank. It streams 704x1280 video with 4 sampling steps and regenerates the starting view after 64-second out-and-back trips. World Labs’ RTFM (blog, October 2025) applies the same principle in a product research preview. It has no explicit 3D: posed frames serve as spatial memory, and nearby frames are “juggled” into the context. It runs at interactive rates on one H100.
  2. Compressed recurrent state. Long-Context State-Space Video World Models (Po et al., preprint, arXiv:2505.20171) uses block-wise state-space layers for long memory and local attention for detail. This is the RSSM idea of post 1 at video scale.
  3. Learned sparse attention over long context. Mixture of Contexts (Cai et al., preprint, arXiv:2508.21058) lets each query route to a few relevant chunks of a long history instead of attending densely.
  4. Explicit 3D memory. Models in this family unproject frames into a point cloud or Gaussians and render that state as conditioning. Examples are GEN3C and Voyager, covered in post 4’s update, and L3G-World (October 2026), whose Gaussian memory grows with the explored scene rather than with time.

A toy run: what a window forgets

The budget figure counts frames; it does not show what forgetting looks like. A toy run does. The “world” is a 1D strip of texture (smoothed noise with unit variance). The camera sees $F = 32$ pixels at a time and moves 2 pixels per step, out and back twice. The toy video model is fit by least squares on a different training strip that is observed with a little noise, and it has two parts. A 3-tap transport kernel moves the visible pixels; because it was fit to noisy frames, it blurs very slightly (weights 0.04, 0.91, 0.04), so errors accumulate with every step. An autoregressive predictor samples each newly revealed pixel from its 4 neighbours. Model $W$ keeps only the current frame, like a sliding window. Model $W + M$ also writes every frame into a memory indexed by world position $x$, which plays the role of the camera-pose retrieval of family 1 or the 3D cache of family 4, and copies from it when old content comes back into view. The kymograph shows the result in space: each row is one frame placed at its world position, so time runs downwards and the zig-zag is the camera path.

The second plot puts a number on it: the RMSE between the current frame and the first rendering of the same world points. This is a consistency score; 0 means “the place looks the same as when you first saw it”.

Read the curves from left to right. Up to step 30 both models only accumulate blur (RMSE 0.16). When the camera turns back, the window model re-imagines every pixel that had left its view, and its error jumps to about 1.3, close to the difference between two unrelated pieces of texture. The memory model stays at 0.24 at step 60 and 0.33 at step 180. What remains is the slow error accumulation of the transport kernel, which grows with the horizon even when retrieval is perfect. The literature makes the same split: retrieval or 3D memory fixes forgetting, while training tricks such as Self Forcing and its successors (post 2’s update) fight accumulation.

A careful 2026 study, Echo-Memory (King et al., preprint, arXiv:2606.09803, June 2026), compares these choices on one shared backbone. It finds that raw context is a strong baseline, that aggressive compression loses the evidence needed to return to a place, and that block-wise state-space recurrence was the best mechanism for returning in open domains. Two surveys organize the area: Towards Interactive Video World Modeling (arXiv:2606.01164) and The Past Frames the Future: Memory for Autoregressive Video Generation (arXiv:2609.28466). A sobering result for all of them comes from post 5’s update. WRBench (June 2026) finds that current models do not keep unseen events evolving. Remembering what a place looked like is not the same as simulating what happened there while you were away.

Physical-AI foundation models and general world models

  • Cosmos, second and third generation. NVIDIA’s World Simulation with Video Foundation Models for Physical AI (preprint, arXiv:2511.00062, October 2025) introduces Cosmos-Predict2.5 and Cosmos-Transfer2.5. The flow-based video model unifies text-, image- and video-to-world generation, and the Transfer model adds control inputs such as depth, segmentation and edges (Cosmos-Transfer1: arXiv:2503.14492). Cosmos-Reason1 (arXiv:2503.15558) is the reasoning vision-language part. On 1 June 2026 NVIDIA announced Cosmos 3 (press release). It is described as an open “omnimodel” with a mixture-of-transformers design: a reasoning transformer plus a generation expert, which understands and generates text, images, video, ambient sound and actions. It can serve as a vision-language model, a world model, or the backbone of a “world action model” for robots. NVIDIA reports leading open-model positions on several leaderboards, including Physics-IQ; treat these as company claims until independent evaluations appear. The design merges the roles of section 5.3 (simulator) with the planner/policy roles of embodied-AI post 5.
  • Runway GWM-1 (Runway, 11 December 2025) is an autoregressive world model built on the Gen-4.5 video model. It generates frame by frame and can be controlled by camera pose, robot commands or audio. It comes in three post-trained variants (Worlds, Avatars, Robotics), with 720p output and videos up to two minutes.
  • Emu3.5 (Cui et al., preprint, arXiv:2510.26583, October 2025) is a native multimodal next-token model trained on interleaved vision-language data, including long video-derived sequences, and presented as a “world learner”. It shows that the autoregressive branch of post 2 is still alive next to diffusion.

Driving world models

Driving remained the most mature industrial use (section 5.2). GAIA-2 (Russell et al., preprint, arXiv:2503.20523, March 2025) is a latent diffusion model that generates consistent multi-camera video. It can be controlled by ego actions, other agents, weather and road layout. Wayve’s GAIA-4 (Wayve blog, 3 August 2026) moves to closed-loop simulation. It reconstructs scenes from recorded sensor logs and keeps the driving policy in the loop, so the policy’s decisions change the camera and radar inputs it receives next. Other road users replay their logged behaviour unless reactive agents are switched on. The Waymo World Model (Waymo blog, 6 February 2026) post-trains Genie 3 for driving and generates both camera and lidar outputs (4D point clouds). It offers driving-action, scene-layout and language control. Waymo presents it as a tool for simulating rare, safety-critical events. This is the clearest case so far of a general world model becoming the base of a domain simulator, the “pre-train broadly, post-train on your car” workflow of section 5.3.

What changed in “What is still missing”

  • Compute and real-time: largely solved at 720p for open models with distillation (tens of fps on one GPU). The open problem is now quality over long streams, not speed.
  • Memory: minute-scale consistency is now an open-model result, not only a frontier claim. Hour-scale consistency and persistent dynamic state (events that continue off-screen) remain unsolved.
  • Action spaces: still fragmented. Camera pose became the de facto shared interface for exploration models, and robot models moved toward joint video-action prediction (post 1’s update).
  • Physics and evaluation: physics benchmarks multiplied, but the best models still score far from perfect (post 5’s update).

For how these models are being wired into game engines (neural rendering in engines, playable neural games, engine-side tooling), see graphics-engine post 6. For the classical simulators they are compared against (Isaac, MuJoCo, Newton and others), see graphics-engine post 5.

Exercises

  1. Run the toy LAM. Then add a second dot that moves randomly and independently of the action. How does code purity change? Propose a modification (hint: give the decoder information about the distractor) that restores it.
  2. Turn the LAM decoder into a multi-step world model and roll it out for 30 steps. Does the dot stay sharp? Implement GameNGen-style context noise augmentation (add noise to the input frame during training and pass the noise level) and compare.
  3. Write down the factorization $p(x_{1:T} \mid a_{1:T})$ used by GameNGen and by Sora-style full-clip diffusion. Which one can be used interactively, and what does each need to be retrained for?
  4. Pick one claim from the Genie 3 announcement and design an experiment (data, metric, baseline) that would test it quantitatively.
  5. (2025-2026 update) Using the numbers in the memory figure, compute the KV-cache size of the full-context model after 120 s if each latent frame has 1,560 tokens, the model has 30 layers, and each layer stores keys and values of width 1,536 in 16-bit floats. Compare it with the $W + K = 46$ frame design.
Hint

Per frame: $1560 \times 30 \times 2 \times 1536 \times 2$ bytes $\approx 0.29$ GB. Full context (720 frames) needs about 207 GB, which does not fit on one GPU. The bounded design (46 frames) needs about 13 GB. This is why every real-time model in the update bounds its context.
