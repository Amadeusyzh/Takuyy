Learning roadmap: from computer graphics to 3D Gaussian Splatting, world models and embodied AI

Created in October 27, 2026

2026   ·   roadmap   ·   roadmap

This post is the map for the rest of the blog. I am a PhD student working at the intersection of embodied AI, world models, AR/VR, HCI and computer graphics, and these fields share one surprisingly linear story: learn how images are formed (graphics), learn how to invert that process from photos (3D vision, then neural rendering and 3D Gaussian Splatting), learn models that predict how the world evolves (world models), and finally put a body inside the world and let it act (embodied AI).

The blog is organised as six series that follow that story, plus a dated paper-digest column. Five series are the stages of the story itself; the sixth, on graphics engines, is the bridge between them, because the simulators that train robots and the neural game engines that world models are becoming are both engines underneath. Every post comes in English, Chinese and Japanese, with the math written out, small runnable code snippets and links to representative papers from SIGGRAPH, CVPR/ICCV/ECCV, NeurIPS/ICLR/ICML, CoRL/RSS and other top venues. Each track also has a curated reading list in the repository.

Stage 0: prerequisites

You do not need everything below before starting, but you will meet all of it:

  • Linear algebra: vectors, matrices, eigen-decomposition, SVD. Much of graphics and vision is $4\times 4$ matrices and least squares.
  • Calculus and probability: integrals over the hemisphere, expectations, Monte Carlo estimation $\hat I = \frac{1}{N}\sum_i f(x_i)/p(x_i)$, Gaussians.
  • Programming: Python with NumPy and PyTorch for most posts; some GLSL and C++ for rendering.

Stage 1: computer graphics from scratch

How an image is made: from transforms and rasterization to physically based light transport and simulation. This is the foundation for everything that follows: NeRF is volume rendering, 3DGS is a differentiable rasterizer, and robot simulators are physics engines plus renderers. Chapters 1-7 are the core. Chapters 8-13 go deeper into geometry, animation, volumes, real-time techniques and differentiable rendering, and end with a survey of the 2026 research frontier; you can read them now or come back to them after the later stages.

  1. Roadmap and the math toolkit
  2. Rasterization
  3. Shading and textures
  4. Physically based rendering
  5. Ray tracing and path tracing
  6. Real-time ray tracing
  7. Physically based simulation
  8. Geometry processing: meshes, curvature, smoothing, subdivision and reconstruction
  9. Animation and characters: splines, rotations, skinning, IK, motion matching and learned motion
  10. Volumes and participating media: fog, smoke, clouds, sky and skin
  11. Real-time rendering techniques: G-buffers, soft shadows, AO, reflections, real-time GI and tone mapping
  12. Differentiable and inverse rendering: gradients through pixels
  13. Graphics research frontiers 2026: what SIGGRAPH, SIGGRAPH Asia and the latest preprints are doing

Stage 1.5: 3D vision

Graphics goes from a scene to an image; 3D vision goes back, from images to geometry. Given photographs, it recovers the cameras that took them (intrinsics, distortion and poses), the correspondences between them and the 3D structure they see. That is exactly what neural rendering takes as given: NeRF and 3DGS are trained on COLMAP poses, and 3DGS starts from the SfM point cloud. Reading this stage first turns those poses from a black box into something you can compute, check and debug when a capture fails, and SLAM, world models and embodied AI build on the same geometry. Posts 1-5 are the core, from the camera model to bundle adjustment; posts 6-10 extend it to real-time tracking, dense depth, representations, reconstruction and AR/VR.

  1. Image formation and camera models: homogeneous coordinates, the pinhole camera $P = K[R \mid \mathbf t]$, lens distortion, and the OpenCV, OpenGL, COLMAP and NeRF conventions.
  2. Camera calibration: estimate $K$ and the distortion from a checkerboard with homographies and Zhang’s method, and judge the result by its reprojection error and uncertainty.
  3. Features and matching: find the same point in two images with corners, SIFT, ORB and learned matchers, and reject wrong matches with RANSAC.
  4. Two-view geometry: the essential and fundamental matrices, relative pose, triangulation, and depth from a rectified stereo pair.
  5. Multi-view geometry and bundle adjustment: PnP, incremental and global Structure-from-Motion, and bundle adjustment with the Schur complement.
  6. Visual SLAM: the same estimation in real time, with factor graphs, visual and visual-inertial odometry, loop closure and pose-graph optimization.
  7. Depth estimation and multi-view stereo: depth sensors, plane sweeps and cost volumes, PatchMatch, learned MVS and monocular depth.
  8. 3D representations: point clouds, meshes, voxels, SDFs, neural fields and 3D Gaussians, how to convert between them and which to choose.
  9. 3D reconstruction: from the classical SfM, MVS and meshing pipeline to neural surfaces and feed-forward models such as DUSt3R and VGGT.
  10. 3D vision for AR/VR: headset tracking, motion-to-photon latency, registration error and spatial anchors, which closes the series.

Stage 2: neural rendering and 3D Gaussian Splatting

Inverting the rendering process: recover cameras from photos (stage 1.5 in depth; the first post below is a compact review), then fit a radiance field (NeRF) or a set of 3D Gaussians (3DGS) that reproduces them. These representations are now the backbone of 3D capture for AR/VR and of many 3D world-generation systems.

  1. Cameras and multi-view geometry
  2. NeRF
  3. Fast radiance fields
  4. 3D Gaussian Splatting
  5. 3DGS frontiers
  6. From capture to VR: a hands-on tutorial

Stage 2.5: graphics engines (the bridge)

A renderer becomes a world that agents can live in only once it sits inside an engine: a game loop, scene data, GPU resources, physics and tools. This series opens up that machinery, from the anatomy of an engine and modern GPU APIs to a mini WebGPU engine you build yourself, the production engines behind games and VR, and the physics engines that double as robot simulators. It ends where the next two stages begin: robot simulators are engines, and neural game engines are world models.

  1. Anatomy of a game engine: loops, data, ECS, jobs and tools
  2. Modern GPU APIs and render graphs
  3. Build a mini engine: WebGPU, glTF, PBR, shadows and an ECS in the browser
  4. Inside production engines: Unreal Engine 5, Unity 6, Godot 4, Bevy and others
  5. Physics and simulation engines: from game physics to robot simulators
  6. AI meets graphics engines: neural rendering, generative content and neural game engines

Stage 3: world models

From reconstructing the world to predicting it: latent dynamics models for planning, the generative toolkit (VAEs, diffusion, flow matching, transformers), video world simulators such as Genie, explicit 3D/4D world generation, and the JEPA view of predictive representation learning.

  1. What is a world model?
  2. Generative modeling foundations
  3. Video world simulators
  4. 3D and 4D world generation
  5. JEPA, evaluation and open problems

Stage 4: embodied AI

Giving the model a body: kinematics and control, reinforcement learning and sim2real, simulators, imitation learning, vision-language-action models, and finally how embodied AI connects with XR and human-computer interaction.

  1. Robotics foundations
  2. Reinforcement learning for robots
  3. Simulation platforms
  4. Imitation learning
