Why this matters

The RL post ended with policies that learn to walk in minutes, but only because thousands of copies of the robot were simulated in parallel. Simulators are to embodied AI what datasets are to computer vision: they generate the training data, define the benchmarks, and their errors become the reality gap. They are also where robotics meets computer graphics most directly. A modern simulator is a game engine with a stricter physics core, and the techniques of the graphics track (rasterization, ray tracing, BVHs, position-based dynamics) are its building blocks. This post explains what a simulator computes, compares the major platforms, and walks through MuJoCo in Python.

Prerequisites

  • Posts 1 and 2 (equations of motion, PD control, RL training loops).
  • Basic numerical integration (explicit and semi-implicit Euler).
  • Optional: the graphics track’s posts on rasterization, ray tracing and physics simulation.

1. What a robotics simulator does

Every simulator repeats the same loop at a fixed time step $\Delta t$ (typically 1 to 5 ms), usually several “substeps” per control step:

flowchart LR
  A[Policy / controller] -->|ctrl| B[Actuator models]
  B --> C[Collision detection]
  C --> D[Constraint and contact solve]
  D --> E[Integrate q, v]
  E --> F[Sensors: joints, IMU, force, touch]
  E --> G[Rendering: RGB, depth, segmentation]
  F --> A
  G --> A

Rigid-body dynamics

Most robotics engines use generalized (reduced) coordinates: the state is the joint vector $q$ and its velocity $v$, and the dynamics are the manipulator equation from post 1,

\[M(q)\,\dot v + c(q, v) = \tau + J_c(q)^\top f_c ,\]

where $c$ collects Coriolis, centrifugal and gravity terms and $J_c^\top f_c$ maps contact forces into joint space. Joints are satisfied exactly by construction, and $M$, $c$ are computed with the $O(n)$ algorithms of Featherstone (CRBA, RNEA, ABA). MuJoCo, Drake, Pinocchio, RaiSim and PhysX articulations work this way. Game engines and many graphics solvers instead use maximal coordinates: every body has its own 6-DoF pose and joints are constraints between bodies. That is simpler and parallelizes well, but joint constraints drift and long chains converge slowly.

Integration is usually semi-implicit (symplectic) Euler, $v_{t+1} = v_t + \Delta t\,\dot v_t$, then $q_{t+1} = q_t \oplus \Delta t\, v_{t+1}$ (where $\oplus$ handles quaternions for free joints). MuJoCo also offers implicit integration of damping terms and an RK4 integrator; stiff joints and actuators often require the implicit options.

Contacts and friction

Contacts are where simulators differ the most and where most sim-to-real problems come from.

  1. Collision detection. A broad phase (bounding-volume hierarchies or sweep-and-prune, exactly the BVH ideas used in ray tracing) finds candidate pairs; a narrow phase computes contact points, normals and penetration depths for convex shapes with GJK/EPA or MPR, or with analytic formulas for primitive pairs (sphere-plane, capsule-capsule). Non-convex meshes are usually decomposed into convex pieces (e.g. V-HACD, CoACD) or approximated with SDFs.

  2. Contact model. For a contact with normal impulse $\lambda_n$ and gap $\phi(q)$ the ideal rigid model is the complementarity condition

    \[0 \le \lambda_n \;\perp\; \phi(q) \ge 0 ,\]

    i.e. either the bodies touch and push, or they separate and the force is zero. Coulomb friction adds a friction cone $\lVert \lambda_t \rVert \le \mu \lambda_n$ together with maximal dissipation. Solving this exactly gives a (nonlinear) complementarity problem per step, often linearized with a pyramidal friction cone into an LCP (Stewart and Trinkle 1996; Anitescu and Potra 1997) and solved with projected Gauss-Seidel (PGS).

  3. MuJoCo’s approach (Todorov, ICRA 2014) relaxes hard complementarity into a convex optimization: constraint forces minimize a quadratic objective subject to friction-cone constraints, with a small amount of softness controlled by the solref and solimp parameters. Convexity gives a unique solution and smooth, invertible dynamics (useful for optimization and estimation) at the cost of small penetrations and slip. You saw a 0.1 mm penetration in the example below.

  4. Position-based approaches. PBD and XPBD (Macklin et al. 2016) skip forces altogether and directly project positions onto constraints. XPBD adds a compliance $\alpha$ so stiffness is independent of the iteration count: for a constraint $C(x) = 0$ with $\tilde\alpha = \alpha / \Delta t^2$, each iteration updates the Lagrange multiplier and positions as

    \[\Delta\lambda = \frac{-C(x) - \tilde\alpha\,\lambda}{\nabla C\, M^{-1}\nabla C^\top + \tilde\alpha}, \qquad \Delta x = M^{-1}\nabla C^\top \Delta\lambda .\]

    This is what the cloth demo on the home page runs, and it is the basis of NVIDIA’s Flex and of cloth, rope and soft-body simulation in Isaac and Genesis.

Practical consequences: contact parameters (friction, restitution, softness) are among the most important things to randomize; small time steps help more than many solver iterations; and grasping with thin objects or many contacts (cloth, granular media, cables) remains hard everywhere.

Actuators and sensors

A real joint is not an ideal torque source. Simulators model actuators: DC motors with gear ratios, armature (reflected rotor inertia, which strongly stabilizes small fast joints), joint damping and friction, torque and velocity limits, position servos with gains, and sometimes learned actuator networks (post 2). Sensors read the state with noise and latency: joint encoders, IMUs (accelerometer and gyro of a site), force/torque sensors, touch sensors on geoms, and cameras.

Rendering

Vision-based policies need images. Options trade speed for realism:

Renderer Examples Use
Rasterization (OpenGL/Vulkan) MuJoCo, SAPIEN, PyBullet, Habitat-Sim fast RGB-D and segmentation, millions of frames
Batched GPU renderers ManiSkill3, Madrona, MJX/MJWarp batch renderers thousands of cameras in parallel for visual RL
Real-time ray/path tracing Isaac Sim (RTX), SAPIEN ray tracer, Genesis photorealistic data, synthetic data generation
Neural / Gaussian-splatting scenes SplatSim, RoboGSim, Real-is-Sim (research) photoreal digital twins reconstructed from real scans

The last row is where the neural-rendering track meets robotics: 3D Gaussian Splatting reconstructs a real workspace from a phone video, and the simulator renders the robot and objects inside it, which can shrink the visual sim-to-real gap.

Scene and robot description formats

  • URDF (ROS): links, joints, inertias, visual and collision meshes; widely supported but tree-only and with no actuators or sensors beyond extensions.
  • MJCF (MuJoCo): richer, including actuators, tendons, sensors, equality constraints and defaults.
  • USD (Pixar’s Universal Scene Description, used by Omniverse/Isaac Sim): full scenes with materials and physics schemas. The graphics pipeline’s scene graph format becomes the robot’s world.

Collections such as MuJoCo Menagerie provide curated, tested models of many commercial robots.

2. The platform landscape

Platform Physics Strengths Typical use
MuJoCo (Google DeepMind, open source since 2022) generalized coords, convex soft contacts accurate, fast on CPU, MJX (JAX) and MuJoCo Warp (GPU) variants, MuJoCo Playground control research, locomotion, dexterous hands, model-based RL
Isaac Sim / Isaac Lab (NVIDIA) PhysX 5 on GPU, RTX rendering, USD massive parallelism, photoreal sensors, large robot library sim-to-real locomotion and manipulation, synthetic data
Habitat (Meta) Bullet-based, focus on rendering speed thousands of FPS in scanned 3D homes (Matterport, HM3D), humans and avatars in 3.0 navigation, rearrangement, embodied QA, human-robot collaboration
SAPIEN / ManiSkill (UCSD, Hillbot) PhysX, GPU-parallel in ManiSkill3 articulated objects (PartNet-Mobility), fast batched rendering, many tasks manipulation benchmarks, visual RL, imitation
Genesis (open-source consortium, 2024) multi-solver: rigid, MPM, SPH, FEM, PBD many material types in one engine, Python-native, generative data engine planned soft bodies, fluids, research prototyping
Drake (TRI/MIT) hydroelastic contact, optimization tools rigorous modelling, trajectory optimization, systems framework model-based control and planning
PyBullet Bullet, maximal + reduced coords simple, ubiquitous in older papers teaching, legacy baselines
Newton (NVIDIA, Google DeepMind, Disney Research; Linux Foundation, 2025) GPU, built on NVIDIA Warp, includes MuJoCo Warp extensible differentiable physics next-generation robot learning

A few specifics worth knowing:

  • Isaac Gym (Makoviychuk et al. 2021) first showed end-to-end GPU simulation and training with tensors that never leave the GPU. It evolved into Orbit (Mittal et al. 2023) and then Isaac Lab, built on Isaac Sim.
  • MJX reimplements MuJoCo in JAX so a whole batch of worlds can be vmapped and jitted onto a GPU or TPU, and is differentiable; MuJoCo Warp is a newer GPU implementation that scales better on contact-rich scenes. MuJoCo Playground (2025) packages locomotion and manipulation environments with sim-to-real recipes on top of them.
  • Habitat optimizes for rendering throughput in photorealistic scanned interiors, which matters for navigation, where physics is simple but images are everything.
  • ManiSkill3 focuses on GPU-parallel simulation and rendering for manipulation, including heterogeneous scenes per environment.

3. Differentiable simulation

If the simulator is differentiable, $\partial s_{t+1}/\partial s_t$ and $\partial s_{t+1}/\partial a_t$ are available, and a policy can be trained by backpropagating through the rollout (analytic policy gradients):

\[\nabla_\theta \sum_{t} r(s_t, a_t), \qquad s_{t+1} = f(s_t, \pi_\theta(s_t)) .\]

Frameworks include Brax (JAX), MJX, DiffTaichi, NVIDIA Warp, and Genesis. In practice contact makes gradients discontinuous or misleading (a tiny change can switch a contact on and produce a huge gradient, or a resting contact can produce zero gradient), and long horizons produce exploding gradients, just as in RNNs. SHAC (Xu et al. ICLR 2022) mitigates this by truncating the rollout into short windows and bootstrapping with a learned critic. Differentiable simulation is most compelling for system identification (fitting friction and mass to real trajectories), soft bodies and cloth, and inverse design; for contact-rich locomotion, sampling-based RL with massive parallelism usually still wins.

A recurring lesson: simulated success rates correlate with real ones only if the simulation was built and validated for that purpose. SimplerEnv explicitly measures that correlation, and it is a good habit to ask of any benchmark.

5. Hands-on: MuJoCo in Python

pip install mujoco gives the engine plus Python bindings. The example defines a 2-link arm and a free box in MJCF, holds the arm at a target with PD control plus bias compensation, and inspects contacts.

import mujoco
import numpy as np

XML = """
<mujoco model="arm_and_box">
  <option timestep="0.002" gravity="0 0 -9.81"/>
  <worldbody>
    <light pos="0 0 3"/>
    <geom name="floor" type="plane" size="2 2 0.1"/>
    <body name="link1" pos="0 0 0.5">
      <joint name="shoulder" type="hinge" axis="0 1 0" damping="0.5"/>
      <geom type="capsule" fromto="0 0 0 0.5 0 0" size="0.04" mass="1"/>
      <body name="link2" pos="0.5 0 0">
        <joint name="elbow" type="hinge" axis="0 1 0" damping="0.5"/>
        <geom type="capsule" fromto="0 0 0 0.4 0 0" size="0.035" mass="0.6"/>
        <site name="ee" pos="0.4 0 0"/>
      </body>
    </body>
    <body name="box" pos="0.6 0.4 0.3">
      <freejoint/>
      <geom name="box" type="box" size="0.05 0.05 0.05" mass="0.2" friction="0.8 0.005 0.0001"/>
    </body>
  </worldbody>
  <actuator>
    <motor name="m_shoulder" joint="shoulder" gear="1" ctrlrange="-30 30"/>
    <motor name="m_elbow" joint="elbow" gear="1" ctrlrange="-30 30"/>
  </actuator>
  <sensor>
    <jointpos joint="shoulder"/>
    <jointpos joint="elbow"/>
    <framepos objtype="site" objname="ee"/>
  </sensor>
</mujoco>
"""

model = mujoco.MjModel.from_xml_string(XML)
data = mujoco.MjData(model)
print("nq", model.nq, "nv", model.nv, "nu", model.nu)   # 2 hinges + 1 free joint: nq=9, nv=8

q_ref = np.array([-0.6, 1.2])                # shoulder, elbow targets (rad)
Kp, Kd = 60.0, 4.0
arm = [model.joint("shoulder").qposadr[0], model.joint("elbow").qposadr[0]]
arm_v = [model.joint("shoulder").dofadr[0], model.joint("elbow").dofadr[0]]

for step in range(1500):                     # 3 s of simulated time
    q, qd = data.qpos[arm], data.qvel[arm_v]
    # PD + gravity/Coriolis compensation: qfrc_bias holds C(q,qd) qd + g(q)
    data.ctrl[:] = Kp * (q_ref - q) - Kd * qd + data.qfrc_bias[arm_v]
    mujoco.mj_step(model, data)

print("joint error", np.round(q_ref - data.qpos[arm], 4))
print("end-effector (sensor)", np.round(data.sensordata[2:5], 3))
print("box height", round(data.body("box").xpos[2], 4))   # resting on the floor at 0.05

# inspect contacts: which geoms touch and with what normal force
f = np.zeros(6)
for i in range(data.ncon):
    c = data.contact[i]
    mujoco.mj_contactForce(model, data, i, f)  # f[0] = normal force in the contact frame
    g1, g2 = model.geom(c.geom1).name, model.geom(c.geom2).name
    print(f"contact {g1}-{g2}: depth {c.dist:+.5f} m, normal force {f[0]:.3f} N")

Output (MuJoCo 3.3.7):

nq 9 nv 8 nu 2
joint error [0. 0.]
end-effector (sensor) [0.743 0.    0.556]
box height 0.0499
contact floor-box: depth -0.00011 m, normal force 0.490 N
contact floor-box: depth -0.00011 m, normal force 0.490 N
contact floor-box: depth -0.00011 m, normal force 0.491 N
contact floor-box: depth -0.00011 m, normal force 0.491 N

Things to notice:

  • nq = 9 but nv = 8: the free joint stores a position and a quaternion (7 numbers) but has only 6 velocity DoFs. Always index with qposadr and dofadr rather than assuming they match.
  • qfrc_bias is exactly $c(q, v)$, so the controller is the “PD + gravity compensation” law from post 1, and the joint error goes to zero.
  • The box rests on four contact points, whose normal forces sum to $4 \times 0.49 \approx 1.96$ N $= 0.2\,\text{kg}\times 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$. The small negative depth (0.1 mm penetration) is MuJoCo’s soft contact at work.
  • To see the scene, run python -m mujoco.viewer and load a file, or call mujoco.viewer.launch_passive(model, data) inside the loop. For offscreen images (e.g. camera observations) use mujoco.Renderer(model, 240, 320), which needs an OpenGL backend such as EGL on headless machines (MUJOCO_GL=egl).

Batched simulation with MJX

The same model runs as a batch of worlds on an accelerator with MJX (pip install mujoco-mjx). Save the example above as arm_and_box.py and:

import jax
import jax.numpy as jnp
import mujoco
from mujoco import mjx

from arm_and_box import XML                  # the MJCF string from the previous example

model = mujoco.MjModel.from_xml_string(XML)
mx = mjx.put_model(model)                    # copy the model to the accelerator

def rollout(qpos0):
    d = mjx.make_data(mx).replace(qpos=qpos0)
    def body(d, _):
        return mjx.step(mx, d), None
    d, _ = jax.lax.scan(body, d, None, length=500)
    return d.qpos

# 1024 worlds whose shoulder angle starts at different values
qpos0 = jnp.tile(jnp.array(model.qpos0), (1024, 1))
qpos0 = qpos0.at[:, 0].set(jnp.linspace(-1.0, 1.0, 1024))
final = jax.jit(jax.vmap(rollout))(qpos0)
print(final.shape)                           # (1024, 9)

vmap turns the single-world step into a batched one and jit compiles it; on a GPU this is the pattern behind “train a locomotion policy in minutes”. Because mjx.step is written in JAX, jax.grad through rollout also works, which is the differentiable-simulation use case above (with the caveats about contact gradients). Importing arm_and_box also runs its CPU simulation once, which is harmless here.

6. Choosing a simulator

  • Locomotion, sim-to-real, many robots in parallel: Isaac Lab, MuJoCo Playground (MJX / MuJoCo Warp), Genesis.
  • Dexterous or precise contact, model-based control, system identification: MuJoCo, Drake.
  • Visual manipulation benchmarks and imitation learning: ManiSkill3, robosuite/robomimic, LIBERO, RLBench.
  • Navigation and home-scale tasks with people: Habitat 3.0, BEHAVIOR-1K (OmniGibson).
  • Deformables, fluids, cloth: Genesis, Isaac Sim (PBD/FEM), dedicated XPBD or MPM code.

Whatever you choose, validate the parts that matter for your task against the real robot: drop tests, swing tests, recorded joint trajectories, and a few real rollouts of the trained policy.

Try it

Open the Lab on the home page and switch to the Cloth · XPBD tab: a 40 x 40 particle cloth simulated with XPBD and many substeps. Pull it, cut it, stretch it until it tears. Every update you see is the $\Delta\lambda$ formula above applied to distance constraints. Notice that more substeps make the cloth stiffer and more stable; that is the same reason robotics simulators prefer small time steps. Neighbouring tabs show the rendering side: a path tracer and a Gaussian-splatting world.

Exercises

  1. In the MuJoCo example, change the box friction to 0.1 and give the floor a slight tilt (rotate the plane). At what angle does the box start sliding? Compare with $\tan\theta = \mu$.
  2. Replace the motor actuators with position actuators (kp gain) and compare the tracking error with and without the qfrc_bias term. Why does a pure position servo sag?
  3. Use mujoco.Renderer to render a 64 x 64 depth image of the scene after each control step, and measure frames per second on CPU.
  4. Randomize the box mass and friction across the 1024 MJX worlds (put per-world values in a batched mjx.Model) and plot the distribution of final box positions after a push.
  5. Implement a 1-D XPBD chain (10 particles, distance constraints) in NumPy and show that increasing the iteration count changes the stiffness with plain PBD but not with XPBD.
