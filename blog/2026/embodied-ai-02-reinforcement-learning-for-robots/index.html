Why this matters

In the previous post every controller was designed by hand from a model. That works for an arm moving in free space, but it becomes very hard for a quadruped running over rubble or a hand spinning a cube, where contacts switch on and off hundreds of times a second. Reinforcement learning (RL) replaces the hand design by optimization: write down a reward, let the robot try millions of times in a simulator, and transfer the resulting policy to hardware. Since about 2019 this recipe has become the standard way to build locomotion controllers, and its ideas (advantages, entropy, domain randomization) show up again in imitation learning, VLA fine-tuning and world-model training later in this series.

1. The RL problem

Markov decision processes

An MDP is a tuple $(\mathcal{S}, \mathcal{A}, P, r, \gamma, \rho_0)$: states, actions, transition dynamics \(P(s' \mid s, a)\), reward $r(s, a)$, discount $\gamma \in [0, 1)$ and initial-state distribution $\rho_0$. A (stochastic) policy $\pi_\theta(a \mid s)$ induces trajectories $\tau = (s_0, a_0, s_1, a_1, \dots)$ and the objective is the expected discounted return

\[J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi_\theta}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty} \gamma^t r(s_t, a_t)\right].\]

Real robots are partially observable (POMDP): the policy sees observations $o_t$ (joint encoders, an IMU, images) rather than the full state (exact contact forces, terrain friction). Practitioners handle this with observation histories, recurrent networks, or the privileged-information tricks of section 4.

For a robot the modelling choices are as important as the algorithm:

MDP element Typical choice for a legged robot
Observation base angular velocity, projected gravity, joint positions and velocities, previous action, velocity command, (height map)
Action joint position targets $q^{\text{des}}$ for a PD loop at 200 Hz to 1 kHz; policy at 50 Hz
Reward track commanded velocity, penalize torque, joint acceleration, action rate, foot slip, base orientation
Episode 20 s, terminate on falls (base contact), randomized start state and terrain

Value functions and the Bellman equations

The state value and action value of a policy are

\[V^\pi(s) = \mathbb{E}_\pi\left[\sum_{k\ge 0}\gamma^k r_{t+k} \,\middle\vert\, s_t = s\right], \qquad Q^\pi(s,a) = \mathbb{E}_\pi\left[\sum_{k\ge 0}\gamma^k r_{t+k} \,\middle\vert\, s_t = s, a_t = a\right],\]

and the advantage $A^\pi(s,a) = Q^\pi(s,a) - V^\pi(s)$ measures how much better action $a$ is than the policy’s average. They satisfy the Bellman expectation equation

\[Q^\pi(s,a) = r(s,a) + \gamma\,\mathbb{E}_{s'\sim P,\,a'\sim\pi}\left[Q^\pi(s',a')\right],\]

and the optimal action value satisfies the Bellman optimality equation \(Q^{\ast}(s,a) = r + \gamma\,\mathbb{E}_{s'}[\max_{a'} Q^{\ast}(s',a')]\). Q-learning and DQN (Mnih et al. 2015) iterate the latter, but the $\max$ over a continuous, high-dimensional action space is awkward, which is why robotics mostly uses policy-gradient and actor-critic methods.

For a small, known MDP the Bellman optimality equation can be solved directly by value iteration, $V_{k+1}(s) = \max_a \sum_{s’} P(s’\mid s,a)\,[r + \gamma V_k(s’)]$. The figure and the widget below show how value spreads backwards from the reward, one sweep at a time.

2. Policy gradients

The policy gradient theorem

Using $\nabla_\theta p_\theta(\tau) = p_\theta(\tau)\nabla_\theta\log p_\theta(\tau)$ and the fact that the dynamics do not depend on $\theta$, one gets (Williams 1992; Sutton et al. 2000)

\[\nabla_\theta J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\pi_\theta}\left[\sum_t \nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)\, \hat A_t\right],\]

where $\hat A_t$ can be the return-to-go (REINFORCE), the return minus a baseline $b(s_t)$, or an advantage estimate. Subtracting a state-dependent baseline does not bias the gradient but greatly reduces its variance. Intuitively, the gradient raises the log-probability of actions that turned out better than expected and lowers the others.

For continuous control the policy is usually a diagonal Gaussian $\pi_\theta(a\mid s) = \mathcal{N}(\mu_\theta(s), \mathrm{diag}(\sigma^2))$ with a state-independent, learned $\log\sigma$.

A one-step problem (a bandit) makes the update visible. Samples with positive advantage pull the mean of the Gaussian toward them, samples with negative advantage push it away, and repeated steps also shrink $\sigma$ once the policy has found the optimum:

Generalized advantage estimation

With a learned critic $V_\phi$ define the TD residual $\delta_t = r_t + \gamma V_\phi(s_{t+1}) - V_\phi(s_t)$. GAE (Schulman et al. 2016) averages $k$-step advantage estimates with exponentially decaying weights:

\[\hat A_t^{\mathrm{GAE}(\gamma,\lambda)} = \sum_{l=0}^{\infty} (\gamma\lambda)^l\,\delta_{t+l}.\]

$\lambda = 0$ gives the low-variance, biased one-step TD advantage; $\lambda = 1$ gives the unbiased, high-variance Monte-Carlo advantage. $\lambda \approx 0.95$ is the usual compromise. The critic is regressed to the targets $\hat R_t = \hat A_t + V_\phi(s_t)$.

Trust regions and PPO

Plain policy gradients are fragile: one large step can collapse the policy, and then the data it collects is useless. TRPO (Schulman et al. 2015) maximizes a surrogate objective subject to a KL-divergence constraint between the old and new policy. PPO (Schulman et al. 2017) gets most of the benefit with a first-order trick. With the probability ratio $\rho_t(\theta) = \pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)/\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(a_t\mid s_t)$ it maximizes

\[L^{\text{CLIP}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_t\left[\min\left(\rho_t(\theta)\hat A_t,\; \operatorname{clip}(\rho_t(\theta), 1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon)\,\hat A_t\right)\right],\]

usually with $\epsilon = 0.2$. The $\min$ makes this a pessimistic bound: once the ratio leaves $[1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon]$ in the direction that would improve the objective, the gradient vanishes, so there is no incentive to move further. That lets PPO reuse each batch for several epochs of minibatch SGD. The full loss adds a value loss and an optional entropy bonus:

\[L(\theta,\phi) = -L^{\text{CLIP}}(\theta) + c_v\,\mathbb{E}_t\left[(V_\phi(s_t) - \hat R_t)^2\right] - c_e\,\mathbb{E}_t\left[\mathcal{H}(\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid s_t))\right].\]

PPO is on-policy and sample-inefficient, but it is simple, stable and embarrassingly parallel. With thousands of simulated robots on one GPU, sample efficiency matters much less than wall-clock time, which is why PPO is the default in Isaac Lab, legged_gym and most locomotion papers.

Plotting $L^{\text{CLIP}}$ for a single sample shows the pessimism of the $\min$: the objective is flat, and its gradient zero, exactly where moving further would look good.

A compact PPO in PyTorch

The following file is self-contained: an actor-critic, GAE, the PPO update, and a vectorized toy task in which 64 point masses must move to the origin (a stand-in for a reaching task). It runs in a few minutes on a laptop CPU.

import torch
import torch.nn as nn

class ActorCritic(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, obs_dim, act_dim, hidden=64):
        super().__init__()
        self.pi = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(obs_dim, hidden), nn.Tanh(),
                                nn.Linear(hidden, hidden), nn.Tanh(), nn.Linear(hidden, act_dim))
        self.v = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(obs_dim, hidden), nn.Tanh(),
                               nn.Linear(hidden, hidden), nn.Tanh(), nn.Linear(hidden, 1))
        self.log_std = nn.Parameter(torch.full((act_dim,), -0.5))

    def dist(self, obs):
        return torch.distributions.Normal(self.pi(obs), self.log_std.exp())

def gae(rew, val, done, last_val, gamma=0.99, lam=0.95):
    """rew, val, done: [T, N] tensors (N parallel envs). Returns advantages and returns."""
    T = rew.shape[0]
    adv = torch.zeros_like(rew)
    next_val, running = last_val, torch.zeros_like(last_val)
    for t in reversed(range(T)):
        mask = 1.0 - done[t]
        delta = rew[t] + gamma * next_val * mask - val[t]
        running = delta + gamma * lam * mask * running
        adv[t] = running
        next_val = val[t]
    return adv, adv + val

def ppo_update(model, opt, obs, act, logp_old, adv, ret,
               clip=0.2, vf_coef=0.5, ent_coef=0.0, epochs=10, minibatch=256):
    """One PPO update on a flattened batch of B transitions."""
    adv = (adv - adv.mean()) / (adv.std() + 1e-8)
    B = obs.shape[0]
    for _ in range(epochs):
        for idx in torch.randperm(B).split(minibatch):
            d = model.dist(obs[idx])
            logp = d.log_prob(act[idx]).sum(-1)
            ratio = (logp - logp_old[idx]).exp()
            surr = torch.min(ratio * adv[idx],
                             ratio.clamp(1 - clip, 1 + clip) * adv[idx])
            v_loss = (model.v(obs[idx]).squeeze(-1) - ret[idx]).pow(2).mean()
            loss = -surr.mean() + vf_coef * v_loss - ent_coef * d.entropy().sum(-1).mean()
            opt.zero_grad()
            loss.backward()
            nn.utils.clip_grad_norm_(model.parameters(), 0.5)
            opt.step()

# --- a toy "reaching" task: a 2-D point mass must move to the origin ---
class PointReach:
    def __init__(self, n, horizon=50):
        self.n, self.H = n, horizon
        self.damp, self.gain = 0.9, 0.1               # nominal physics
    def reset(self):
        self.pos = torch.rand(self.n, 2) * 2 - 1
        self.vel = torch.zeros(self.n, 2)
        self.t = torch.zeros(self.n)
        return torch.cat([self.pos, self.vel], -1)
    def step(self, a):
        a = a.clamp(-1, 1)
        self.vel = self.damp * self.vel + self.gain * a  # simple damped dynamics
        self.pos = self.pos + 0.1 * self.vel
        self.t += 1
        rew = -self.pos.norm(dim=-1) - 0.01 * a.pow(2).sum(-1)
        done = (self.t >= self.H).float()
        if done.any():                                # auto-reset finished envs
            k = done.bool()
            self.pos[k] = torch.rand(int(k.sum()), 2) * 2 - 1
            self.vel[k] = 0.0
            self.t[k] = 0
        return torch.cat([self.pos, self.vel], -1), rew, done

torch.manual_seed(0)
env, T = PointReach(n=64), 128
model = ActorCritic(4, 2)
opt = torch.optim.Adam(model.parameters(), lr=3e-4)
obs = env.reset()
for it in range(150):
    buf = {k: [] for k in ["obs", "act", "logp", "rew", "val", "done"]}
    with torch.no_grad():
        for t in range(T):
            d = model.dist(obs)
            a = d.sample()
            buf["obs"].append(obs); buf["act"].append(a)
            buf["logp"].append(d.log_prob(a).sum(-1))
            buf["val"].append(model.v(obs).squeeze(-1))
            obs, r, done = env.step(a)
            buf["rew"].append(r); buf["done"].append(done)
        last_val = model.v(obs).squeeze(-1)
    b = {k: torch.stack(v) for k, v in buf.items()}            # [T, N, ...]
    adv, ret = gae(b["rew"], b["val"], b["done"], last_val)
    flat = lambda x: x.reshape(T * env.n, *x.shape[2:])
    ppo_update(model, opt, flat(b["obs"]), flat(b["act"]), flat(b["logp"]), flat(adv), flat(ret))
    if it % 25 == 0 or it == 149:
        print(f"iter {it:3d}  mean reward per step {b['rew'].mean():.3f}")

On my run the mean reward per step goes from about $-0.89$ (random actions) to about $-0.1$ (the masses reach the origin and stay there). Details that matter in practice and are easy to get wrong:

  • Masking at episode ends. mask stops bootstrapping across an auto-reset. Strictly, a time-limit truncation should still bootstrap from $V(s_{T})$ (the robot did not fail, we just stopped watching); legged_gym handles this by adding $\gamma V$ of the final state to the reward on time-outs. The toy code treats time-outs as terminal, which is a small bias.
  • Advantage normalization, gradient clipping and a state-independent log-std are standard; observation normalization (running mean/variance) matters on real robots where units differ by orders of magnitude.
  • Action scaling. The network output is clamped to $[-1, 1]$ here; on a robot it is usually scaled and added to a nominal joint configuration, $q^{\text{des}} = q_0 + k_a a_t$.

3. Soft Actor-Critic

PPO throws away data after each update. Off-policy methods keep a replay buffer and are far more sample-efficient, which matters when samples come from a real robot. SAC (Haarnoja et al. 2018) optimizes the maximum-entropy objective

\[J(\pi) = \sum_t \mathbb{E}_{(s_t,a_t)\sim\pi}\left[r(s_t,a_t) + \alpha\,\mathcal{H}(\pi(\cdot\mid s_t))\right],\]

which keeps the policy as random as possible while still solving the task. This gives robust exploration and a policy that does not collapse onto one brittle solution. SAC learns two soft Q-functions $Q_{\phi_1}, Q_{\phi_2}$ (taking the minimum to fight overestimation, as in TD3) with targets

\[y = r + \gamma\left(\min_{j=1,2} Q_{\bar\phi_j}(s', \tilde a') - \alpha\log\pi_\theta(\tilde a'\mid s')\right), \qquad \tilde a' \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot\mid s'),\]

where $\bar\phi$ are slowly updated (Polyak-averaged) target networks. The actor uses the reparameterization trick with a tanh-squashed Gaussian, $\tilde a = \tanh(\mu_\theta(s) + \sigma_\theta(s)\odot\xi)$ with $\xi\sim\mathcal{N}(0, I)$, and minimizes

\[L_\pi(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{s\sim\mathcal{D},\,\xi}\left[\alpha\log\pi_\theta(\tilde a\mid s) - \min_j Q_{\phi_j}(s, \tilde a)\right].\]

The tanh changes the density, so $\log\pi(\tilde a\mid s) = \log\mathcal{N}(u;\mu,\sigma) - \sum_i\log(1-\tanh^2 u_i)$. The follow-up paper (Haarnoja et al. 2018b) also tunes $\alpha$ automatically toward a target entropy (often $-\dim\mathcal{A}$) and demonstrated learning to walk directly on a real Minitaur quadruped in about two hours of experience.

  PPO SAC
Data on-policy, discard after update off-policy, replay buffer
Sample efficiency low high
Parallel simulation scales very well needs care (update-to-data ratio)
Typical use sim-to-real locomotion, massively parallel sim real-world RL, manipulation, fine-tuning
Exploration Gaussian noise + optional entropy bonus maximum entropy, automatic temperature

4. Reward design

The reward is the specification of the task, and every loophole will be found. Common patterns:

  • Dense task terms (e.g. \(\exp(-\lVert v^{\text{cmd}}_{xy} - v_{xy}\rVert^2 / \sigma)\) for velocity tracking) plus many small regularization penalties (torque, power, joint acceleration, action rate $\lVert a_t - a_{t-1}\rVert^2$, foot slip, feet air time). The penalties are what make gaits look natural and transferable; without them policies vibrate, which simulators tolerate and motors do not.
  • Potential-based shaping (Ng, Harada and Russell, ICML 1999): adding \(F = \gamma\Phi(s') - \Phi(s)\) for any potential $\Phi$ provably leaves the optimal policy unchanged, while other shaping terms can change it.
  • Sparse rewards plus demonstrations or curricula for manipulation: success/failure is easy to specify but hard to explore, so one adds demonstrations (post 4), automatic curricula (terrain difficulty that increases as the policy improves, as in Rudin et al.), or goal relabeling.
  • Style rewards from data: motion imitation (DeepMimic, Peng et al. 2018) or adversarial motion priors reward the policy for looking like reference motion capture, a direct bridge from character animation to robotics.
  • LLM-written rewards: Eureka (Ma et al. 2024) asks an LLM to write reward code, trains policies in Isaac Gym, and feeds training statistics back to the LLM to refine the reward.

5. Sim-to-real transfer

Policies trained in simulation face the reality gap: wrong masses and friction, unmodelled actuator dynamics, latency, sensor noise, and images that look nothing like rendered ones. The main tools:

Domain randomization

Instead of making the simulator exactly right, make it randomly wrong in many ways, so that the real world looks like just another sample. Tobin et al. (IROS 2017) randomized textures, lighting, camera pose and distractors in a non-photorealistic renderer and trained an object detector that localized objects on a real robot to within about 1.5 cm with no real images. Peng et al. (ICRA 2018) randomized dynamics (masses, friction, damping, latency) and used a recurrent policy that can implicitly identify the current dynamics. OpenAI’s dexterous hand work (2018, 2019) pushed this to the extreme with automatic domain randomization, gradually widening the ranges as the policy succeeds.

In code, randomization is just per-environment physics parameters resampled on reset. In the toy task above:

class RandomizedPointReach(PointReach):
    """Domain randomization: every env gets its own damping and actuator gain."""
    def reset(self):
        self.damp = torch.empty(self.n, 1).uniform_(0.8, 0.97)
        self.gain = torch.empty(self.n, 1).uniform_(0.05, 0.2)
        return super().reset()

Replace PointReach(n=64) by RandomizedPointReach(n=64) and the same PPO loop learns a single policy that works across all of these “robots”. (For brevity the parameters are sampled once per reset(); a full implementation resamples them for each environment when it auto-resets.) The trade-off is conservatism: the wider the ranges, the more robust and the less optimal the policy.

System identification and actuator models

Randomization is no substitute for getting the big things right. Hwangbo et al. (Science Robotics 2019) trained an actuator network, an MLP that maps a short history of joint position errors and velocities to the torque the real series-elastic actuator produces, on data recorded from ANYmal. Plugging it into the simulator closed most of the gap; the learned policies ran faster than the previous model-based controller and could recover from falls in complex configurations, which hand-designed controllers had not achieved.

Privileged teachers, students and adaptation

A simulator knows things the robot never will: exact terrain, friction, contact states. Two widespread ways to use that knowledge:

  • Asymmetric actor-critic (Pinto et al. RSS 2018): the critic sees the full simulator state while the actor sees only the robot’s observations. The critic is discarded at deployment, so it may cheat.
  • Teacher-student distillation (Lee et al. Science Robotics 2020): first train a teacher policy with privileged information, then train a student that sees only proprioceptive history to imitate the teacher. The student learns to infer the terrain from how its legs feel. This let ANYmal walk over mud, snow, rubble and vegetation it never saw in training. Miki et al. (2022) extended it with an attention-based fusion of exteroception (height maps from cameras and LiDAR) and proprioception, and the robot completed a hiking trail up Mount Etzel in Switzerland.
  • Rapid Motor Adaptation (Kumar et al. RSS 2021): encode the privileged environment parameters into a latent “extrinsics” vector, and train an adaptation module to estimate it online from recent states and actions.

Massively parallel simulation

Rudin et al. (CoRL 2021) showed that running thousands of environments on a single GPU with Isaac Gym, combined with a game-inspired terrain curriculum and carefully tuned PPO, trains a quadruped policy for flat terrain in under four minutes and for uneven terrain in about twenty, and the policies transfer to the real ANYmal. Their open-source legged_gym became the template for a generation of locomotion work, including humanoids (Radosavovic et al., Science Robotics 2024). The lesson: when simulation is cheap, the simple on-policy algorithm wins.

6. Beyond locomotion

  • Manipulation. End-to-end visuomotor policies (Levine et al. JMLR 2016) trained with guided policy search; QT-Opt grasping from large-scale real data; in-hand reorientation with a Shadow hand (OpenAI 2018). Manipulation is harder for RL than locomotion because rewards are sparse and the relevant state (object pose, contacts) is hard to perceive, which is why imitation learning (post 4) dominates manipulation today.
  • Agile flight. Champion-level drone racing (Kaufmann et al. Nature 2023) combined RL in simulation with data-driven residual models of perception and dynamics.
  • RL fine-tuning of foundation policies. Increasingly, RL is used to refine a policy pre-trained by imitation, using success rewards or learned reward models; the ideas of this post carry over directly.

Exercises

  1. Derive the policy gradient theorem from $\nabla_\theta\log p_\theta(\tau)$ and show that a state-dependent baseline leaves it unbiased.
  2. In the PPO code, set lam=0 and lam=1 in gae and compare learning curves. Then set clip=10 (effectively no clipping) and observe the stability.
  3. Fix the time-limit bias: return separate terminated and truncated flags from PointReach.step and bootstrap from the value of the final observation on truncation.
  4. Train on PointReach, then evaluate on RandomizedPointReach with extreme parameters, and compare against a policy trained with randomization. Add the true damping to the observation (a “privileged” input) and see how much it helps.
  5. Implement SAC for the same task (replay buffer, twin Q, tanh Gaussian, automatic $\alpha$) and compare the number of environment steps needed with PPO.
