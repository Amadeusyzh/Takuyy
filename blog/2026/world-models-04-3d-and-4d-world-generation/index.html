Why this matters

The video world simulators of post 3 keep the whole world implicitly inside a network and its context window; that is why they forget what is behind you. The opposite design keeps an explicit 3D (or 4D) representation, such as a mesh, a NeRF or a set of 3D Gaussians, and uses generative models only to create or extend it. Once the world exists in 3D, a classical renderer guarantees multi-view consistency, it can be edited object by object, run in real time on a phone or a VR headset, and handed to a physics engine. For readers coming from the Neural Rendering & 3DGS series, this post is where that series meets world models: the representation is the one you already know, and the new question is how to generate it from text or a single image.

Prerequisites

  • Diffusion models, classifier-free guidance and DDIM (post 2).
  • Radiance fields and 3D Gaussian Splatting (Kerbl et al., SIGGRAPH 2023 / ACM TOG 42(4)): differentiable rendering $x = g(\theta, \text{camera})$.
  • Pinhole camera model: intrinsics $K$, camera-to-world pose $[R \mid t]$, unprojection with depth.

1. The core difficulty: no 3D data at internet scale

There are billions of captioned images and videos but only a few million 3D assets and very few large, high-quality 3D scenes. Every method in this post therefore borrows a 2D prior (an image or video diffusion model) and lifts it to 3D. There are four main strategies:

Strategy Idea Examples Speed
Score distillation optimize a 3D representation so that all its renderings look likely under a 2D diffusion model DreamFusion, Magic3D, ProlificDreamer, DreamGaussian minutes to hours per asset
Multi-view generation + reconstruction generate several consistent views with a 3D-aware diffusion model, then reconstruct Zero-1-to-3, MVDream, CAT3D about a minute
Feed-forward 3D a large network directly outputs a 3D representation from an image LRM and successors seconds
Scene growing (inpaint + depth) render the current scene from a new camera, inpaint the holes with a 2D model, lift with monocular depth, fuse Text2Room, LucidDreamer, WonderJourney, WonderWorld seconds to hours per scene

Objects are mostly handled by the first three; scenes and worlds, which are unbounded and need room-scale layout, mostly by the fourth, or by systems that combine all of them (Marble, Section 6).

2. Score distillation sampling (SDS)

2.1 Derivation

DreamFusion (Poole et al., ICLR 2023, arXiv:2209.14988) optimizes NeRF parameters $\theta$ so that a rendering $x = g(\theta)$ from a random camera looks like a sample from a frozen text-to-image diffusion model $\hat\epsilon_\phi(x_t; y, t)$ with prompt $y$. The natural idea is to minimize the diffusion training loss with respect to the image instead of the network:

\[\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{diff}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{t,\epsilon}\Big[w(t)\,\big\lVert \hat\epsilon_\phi(\alpha_t x + \sigma_t \epsilon;\ y, t) - \epsilon \big\rVert^2\Big], \qquad x = g(\theta).\]

Its gradient contains the Jacobian of the U-Net, $\partial \hat\epsilon_\phi / \partial x_t$, which is expensive and, as the paper found, works poorly. Dropping it yields the SDS gradient:

\[\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SDS}} = \mathbb{E}_{t,\epsilon}\Big[w(t)\,\big(\hat\epsilon_\phi(x_t;\ y, t) - \epsilon\big)\,\frac{\partial x}{\partial \theta}\Big].\]

Intuitively: add noise to the rendering, ask the diffusion model which noise it “sees”, and push the rendering in the direction that makes the residual $(\hat\epsilon - \epsilon)$ small. The paper shows this is the gradient of a weighted sum of KL divergences between the noised rendering distribution $q(x_t \mid x = g(\theta))$ and the model’s noisy marginal $p_\phi(x_t \mid y)$. Since $\hat\epsilon_\phi \approx -\sigma_t \nabla \ln p_t$ (post 2, Section 5.3), SDS is gradient ascent on the log-density of the noised rendering, averaged over noise levels: it is mode seeking.

2.2 Practical consequences

  • DreamFusion used a very large classifier-free guidance weight ($w = 100$) to get sharp results. Combined with mode seeking, this causes the typical over-saturated, over-smoothed look of SDS assets and low diversity across seeds.
  • The 2D prior has no notion of 3D identity, so each view tries to look like the “canonical” view of the object; the result can have several faces (the Janus problem). View-dependent prompts (“front view”, “back view”) help; 3D-aware priors (Section 3) fix it more fundamentally.
  • Magic3D (Lin et al., CVPR 2023) speeds this up with a coarse-to-fine schedule (a coarse neural field, then a textured mesh optimized with a latent diffusion prior at high resolution).
  • ProlificDreamer (Wang et al., NeurIPS 2023) proposes variational score distillation (VSD): treat the 3D scene as a random variable and replace the noise $\epsilon$ in the SDS residual by the prediction of a second, LoRA-fine-tuned diffusion model that tracks the distribution of the current renderings:
\[\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{VSD}} = \mathbb{E}_{t,\epsilon,c}\Big[w(t)\,\big(\hat\epsilon_{\phi}(x_t;\ y, t) - \hat\epsilon_{\mathrm{lora}}(x_t;\ y, t, c)\big)\,\frac{\partial x}{\partial \theta}\Big],\]

where $c$ is the camera. This works with normal guidance weights (e.g. 7.5) and gives far more detailed and diverse results.

  • DreamGaussian (Tang et al., ICLR 2024) and GaussianDreamer (Yi et al., CVPR 2024) replace NeRF by 3D Gaussians, which renders much faster and makes densification a natural way to grow geometry; DreamGaussian produces a textured mesh in about two minutes.

2.3 A runnable toy: SDS is mode seeking

The script below trains a small 2D diffusion model on a mixture of 8 Gaussians (exactly as in post 2), then treats 512 free 2D points as the “scene” $\theta$, with the identity as the “renderer” $g$, and optimizes them by SDS. Compare the spread of the optimized points with the spread of real samples.

import math
import torch
import torch.nn as nn

torch.manual_seed(0)
T = 1000
betas = torch.linspace(1e-4, 0.02, T)
alpha_bar = torch.cumprod(1 - betas, dim=0)
ang = torch.arange(8).float() * (2 * math.pi / 8)
CENTERS = 2.0 * torch.stack([torch.cos(ang), torch.sin(ang)], dim=1)

def sample_data(n):
    return CENTERS[torch.randint(0, 8, (n,))] + 0.1 * torch.randn(n, 2)

def temb(t, dim=32):
    freqs = torch.exp(-math.log(10000.0) * torch.arange(dim // 2) / (dim // 2))
    a = t.float()[:, None] * freqs[None]
    return torch.cat([torch.sin(a), torch.cos(a)], 1)

net = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(34, 256), nn.SiLU(), nn.Linear(256, 256), nn.SiLU(),
                    nn.Linear(256, 256), nn.SiLU(), nn.Linear(256, 2))
eps_model = lambda x, t: net(torch.cat([x, temb(t)], 1))

# 1) "pretrain" the 2D prior with the DDPM loss
opt = torch.optim.Adam(net.parameters(), lr=1e-3)
for step in range(4000):
    x0, t = sample_data(512), torch.randint(0, T, (512,))
    eps = torch.randn_like(x0)
    ab = alpha_bar[t][:, None]
    loss = ((eps_model(ab.sqrt() * x0 + (1 - ab).sqrt() * eps, t) - eps) ** 2).mean()
    opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); opt.step()
net.requires_grad_(False)

# 2) score distillation: theta are free parameters, "rendering" x = g(theta) = theta
theta = (0.5 * torch.randn(512, 2)).requires_grad_(True)
opt = torch.optim.Adam([theta], lr=1e-2)
for step in range(2000):
    t_max = int(980 - 930 * step / 2000)                      # anneal the noise level from high to low
    t = torch.randint(20, t_max, (512,))
    eps = torch.randn_like(theta)
    ab = alpha_bar[t][:, None]
    with torch.no_grad():
        x_t = ab.sqrt() * theta + (1 - ab).sqrt() * eps
        grad = eps_model(x_t, t) - eps                       # w(t) = 1
    opt.zero_grad()
    theta.grad = grad                                         # SDS: skip the network Jacobian
    opt.step()

def spread(x):
    d = torch.cdist(x, CENTERS)
    idx = d.argmin(1)
    return (x - CENTERS[idx]).norm(dim=1).mean().item(), idx.unique().numel()

with torch.no_grad():
    s_real, m_real = spread(sample_data(512))
    s_sds, m_sds = spread(theta)
print(f"real data : mean distance to nearest mode {s_real:.3f}, modes covered {m_real}/8")
print(f"SDS points: mean distance to nearest mode {s_sds:.3f}, modes covered {m_sds}/8")

The SDS points end up much closer to the mode centers than real samples are: each point climbs to a nearby density peak instead of reproducing the spread of the distribution. In 3D this is exactly the over-smoothed, “average-looking” texture of SDS assets. (Which modes are covered depends on the initialization, because each particle simply climbs the nearest hill.) Replacing $\epsilon$ in grad by the prediction of a second model trained on the current particles turns this into a toy VSD. Note also the annealed noise schedule in the loop. If $t$ is drawn uniformly over the whole range at every step, the high-noise terms dominate; the noisy marginal at large $t$ is a single blob around the data mean, so the particles are pulled toward the center of the circle instead of onto the modes (try it). Lowering the maximum timestep during optimization, as ProlificDreamer does with its two-stage time schedule, first settles the coarse layout and then the details.

3. 3D-aware priors: multi-view diffusion and feed-forward models

Instead of fighting the 2D prior’s lack of 3D awareness, one can fine-tune it on 3D data so that it understands cameras:

  • Zero-1-to-3 (Liu et al., ICCV 2023) fine-tunes Stable Diffusion to synthesize a novel view of an object given an input image and a relative camera transform; used as an SDS prior it greatly reduces the Janus problem.
  • MVDream (Shi et al., ICLR 2024) generates several views jointly with cross-view attention, so the views are consistent with each other.
  • CAT3D (Gao et al., NeurIPS 2024) generates many consistent novel views of a scene from one or a few images with a multi-view diffusion model, then reconstructs a NeRF-like representation from them, in about a minute.
  • LRM (Hong et al., ICLR 2024) is a large Transformer that maps a single image to a triplane NeRF in a few seconds, trained on large 3D object datasets; many follow-ups output 3D Gaussians directly.
  • Pose-free reconstruction such as DUSt3R (Wang et al., CVPR 2024) predicts dense 3D point maps from image pairs without known cameras, which makes it easy to lift generated images into a common 3D frame.

The general lesson: generate in 2D where the data is, reconstruct in 3D where consistency is needed. That is also how scene generators work.

Update (November 2026): DUSt3R’s successors (VGGT, π³, MapAnything, Depth Anything 3) now predict cameras and geometry for many views in one pass. Several 2025-2026 generators decode 3D Gaussians or triangles directly from video-model latents. See the 2025-2026 update section near the end of this post.

4. Scene generation by iterative inpainting and lifting

4.1 The loop

Text2Room (Höllein et al., ICCV 2023, arXiv:2303.11989) was the first to generate a room-scale textured mesh from text alone. The loop, shared by most later scene generators, is:

  1. Render the current scene from a new camera pose. Pixels not covered by existing geometry form a mask $M$.
  2. Inpaint the masked region with a text-conditioned 2D inpainting model (Stable Diffusion inpainting).
  3. Estimate depth for the new image with a monocular depth network.
  4. Align the predicted depth to the depth of the existing geometry on the known pixels (monocular depth is only defined up to scale and shift).
  5. Unproject the new pixels into 3D and fuse them into the scene (a mesh in Text2Room; points or Gaussians in later work).

Drawn as a cycle:

Text2Room splits this into a generation stage with carefully chosen camera trajectories, which lays out the room, and a completion stage that picks additional viewpoints to fill the remaining holes. Viewpoint selection matters: bad trajectories produce stretched geometry at depth discontinuities.

4.2 The geometry, with code

Depth alignment is a least-squares problem over the known pixels $\mathcal{K}$:

\[(s^\ast, b^\ast) = \arg\min_{s, b} \sum_{p \in \mathcal{K}} \big(s\, \hat d_p + b - d_p\big)^2,\]

after which a pixel $(u, v)$ with aligned depth $d = s^\ast \hat d + b^\ast$ is unprojected to the world as

\[X_{\mathrm{world}} = R\, \big(d\, K^{-1} [u, v, 1]^\top\big) + t .\] 
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
H, W = 48, 64
K = np.array([[60.0, 0, W / 2], [0, 60.0, H / 2], [0, 0, 1]])

# ground-truth depth of a tilted plane (stands in for the true scene)
v, u = np.mgrid[0:H, 0:W]
d_true = 3.0 + 0.02 * u + 0.01 * v

# monocular depth = unknown affine transform of the truth + noise (relative depth)
d_mono = (d_true - 1.2) / 2.5 + 0.01 * rng.standard_normal((H, W))

# pixels already covered by the existing scene (left half) have known depth
known = u < W // 2
A = np.stack([d_mono[known], np.ones(known.sum())], axis=1)
s, b = np.linalg.lstsq(A, d_true[known], rcond=None)[0]
d_aligned = s * d_mono + b
print(f"scale {s:.3f}, shift {b:.3f}; error on NEW pixels: "
      f"{np.abs(d_aligned - d_true)[~known].mean():.4f} (unaligned {np.abs(d_mono - d_true)[~known].mean():.3f})")

def unproject(depth, K, R=np.eye(3), t=np.zeros(3), mask=None):
    """Lift pixels to world-space points: X = R (d K^-1 [u, v, 1]) + t."""
    vv, uu = np.mgrid[0:depth.shape[0], 0:depth.shape[1]]
    pix = np.stack([uu, vv, np.ones_like(uu)], axis=-1).reshape(-1, 3).astype(float)
    rays = pix @ np.linalg.inv(K).T                 # camera-space rays with z = 1
    pts = rays * depth.reshape(-1, 1)
    pts = pts @ R.T + t
    return pts if mask is None else pts[mask.reshape(-1)]

new_points = unproject(d_aligned, K, mask=~known)   # only the inpainted (new) region
print("new points:", new_points.shape, "z range:", new_points[:, 2].min().round(2), new_points[:, 2].max().round(2))

In a real pipeline the alignment is often done per region or with a depth-completion network conditioned on the known depth, because a single global affine fit cannot repair a wrong depth shape. WonderWorld (below) calls its version guided depth diffusion.

4.3 From meshes to Gaussians, and from rooms to journeys

  • LucidDreamer (Chung et al., arXiv:2311.13384, 2023; later in IEEE TVCG) runs a “dreaming” step (inpaint + depth from a new view) and an “alignment” step that merges the new points into the existing point cloud, then uses the point cloud to initialize 3D Gaussian Splatting and optimizes it on the generated images. Its input can be text, RGB or RGB-D, and the result is a domain-free scene that renders in real time. (Not to be confused with the text-to-3D object method of the same name by Liang et al., CVPR 2024.)
  • WonderJourney (Yu et al., CVPR 2024, arXiv:2312.03884) generates a journey through a long sequence of diverse but connected 3D scenes, starting from an image or text. A large language model writes the description of the next scene, a visual module builds it as a point cloud with inpainting and depth, and a vision-language model checks each result for artifacts and triggers regeneration.
  • WonderWorld (Yu et al., CVPR 2025, arXiv:2406.09394) makes this interactive: a user moves the camera and types what should appear, and a new connected scene is generated in under 10 seconds on a single A6000 GPU. It uses FLAGS (fast layered Gaussian surfels), a geometry-based initialization that needs only a single view and little optimization, layer-wise generation to handle disocclusions, and guided depth diffusion for consistent depth. The authors note that scenes only have front-facing surfaces, so the viewing range is limited.

This family is the closest explicit analog of the interactive video world models of post 3: it is driven by a camera and text, and it grows the world as you explore, but what it grows is a persistent 3D asset.

5. 4D: adding time

A 4D world is a 3D representation plus dynamics. Two common representations: a dynamic NeRF (a time-conditioned field), and deformable 3D Gaussians, where canonical Gaussians are moved by a deformation field $\Delta\mu, \Delta q, \Delta s = D_\psi(\mu, t)$ (as in 4D Gaussian Splatting, Wu et al., CVPR 2024, arXiv:2310.08528, which renders dynamic captured scenes in real time).

Generation again borrows priors, now including video diffusion models:

  • MAV3D (Singer et al., ICML 2023, arXiv:2301.11280) was the first text-to-4D method: a dynamic NeRF optimized with SDS from a text-to-video model.
  • 4D-fy (Bahmani et al., CVPR 2024, arXiv:2311.17984) observes a trade-off: image models give good appearance and 3D structure but no motion, video models give motion but weaker appearance and structure. Hybrid SDS alternates gradients from both kinds of priors (plus a 3D-aware prior).
  • Align Your Gaussians (Ling et al., CVPR 2024, arXiv:2312.13763) uses dynamic 3D Gaussians with deformation fields and composes text-to-image, text-to-video and multi-view diffusion feedback, with an autoregressive scheme to chain longer sequences.
  • Physics as the dynamics model. Instead of distilling motion from video models, one can simulate it: PhysGaussian (Xie et al., CVPR 2024, arXiv:2311.12198) treats Gaussians as continuum particles and integrates them with the material point method, so that the same primitives are both simulated and rendered. This is the explicit-world answer to “video models do not know physics”.

6. World Labs’ Marble and productized world generators

World Labs released Marble as a limited beta in September 2025 and made it publicly available on November 12, 2025. Marble generates persistent, downloadable 3D worlds from text, images, video or coarse 3D layouts, rather than generating frames on the fly. According to the launch post, Gaussian splats are the highest-fidelity representation of a Marble world, and worlds can also be exported as meshes (low-fidelity collider meshes for physics and high-quality meshes). An experimental editor called Chisel lets users block out structure with simple shapes or imported assets and have the model fill in the appearance. No technical paper describes the model, so how it works internally is not public.

Update (November 2026): World Labs added a World API (January 2026) and the Spark 2.0 web renderer (April 2026). In September 2026 it announced Atlas, a multimodal model that will power future Marble versions. See the update section.

Marble is useful as a reference point because it embodies the explicit route end to end: generation produces an asset (splats + meshes) that is then consumed by ordinary graphics and game tooling. The pixel-space route (Genie 3) instead produces an experience that exists only while the model runs.

7. Trade-offs: pixels vs. explicit 3D/4D

Criterion Pixel-space video world model (post 3) Explicit 3D / 4D world
Multi-view consistency learned, approximate; degrades with time guaranteed by the renderer for static content
Memory / persistence limited by context window the representation is the memory
Dynamics, agents, effects rich, learned from video (water, smoke, characters) hard: need 4D generation or an explicit simulator
Physics implicit, often wrong out of distribution can plug into physics engines (meshes, MPM on Gaussians), but materials must be inferred
Editability via prompts / world events, not precise per-object edits, sculpting, relighting, export to engines
Rendering cost at runtime a large network per frame (GPUs/TPUs) rasterization or splatting, real time on consumer hardware and VR
Generality of content anything seen in video mostly static scenes; unbounded and dynamic content is harder
Interactivity with actions native (action-conditioned) requires a game engine or simulator around the asset

The two routes are converging. Video models are being conditioned on explicit 3D signals (camera trajectories, point clouds, depth) to improve consistency, and explicit pipelines increasingly use video models as their prior (CAT3D-style multi-view generation, 4D-fy). A plausible research direction is a hybrid world model: an explicit, persistent 3D/4D state that is updated by learned dynamics and rendered either by splatting or by a generative “neural renderer” for effects the explicit state cannot represent.

8. Try it: an editable explicit world

The Gaussian World tab of the lab on the home page is a toy illustration of the explicit route. Its world is made of around 200,000 3D Gaussians rendered with the 3DGS pipeline (EWA projection, depth sort, alpha compositing). You can type a prompt or pick a preset, then sculpt the terrain, paint materials, place or erase objects, flood the valley, change the time of day and walk through it in first person.

Two honest caveats. First, the generator is procedural, not learned: a keyword-driven program reads the prompt and builds terrain, lakes, forests and villages; a real system would replace it with a learned model such as the scene generators in Section 4. Second, its dynamics (wind, seasons, relighting) are hand-written shaders, not learned. What the demo does show well is the benefit of the explicit route: every edit is local, persistent and immediately consistent from every viewpoint, and rendering runs in real time in a browser, which is precisely what pixel-space world models struggle with.

Feed-forward geometry became the backbone

Section 3 ended with DUSt3R: point maps from image pairs without known cameras. In 2025 this idea scaled into general feed-forward geometry models. Their output is exactly what scene generators need to turn generated pixels into a persistent 3D state.

  • VGGT (Wang et al., CVPR 2025, arXiv:2503.11651) is one large transformer that, given one to hundreds of views, predicts cameras, depth maps, point maps and point tracks in a single forward pass. It needs no test-time optimization.
  • π³ (Wang et al., preprint, arXiv:2507.13347) makes the network permutation-equivariant, so no input view is privileged as the reference frame. MapAnything (Keetha et al., 3DV 2026, arXiv:2509.13414) predicts metric geometry and can use optional extra inputs such as intrinsics, poses or depth. Depth Anything 3 (Lin et al., preprint, arXiv:2511.10647) shows that a plain transformer predicting depth and ray maps from any number of views is enough for consistent geometry.

Why this matters for world generation: the depth-alignment step of section 4.2 (fitting monocular depth to the existing scene) can now be replaced by one network call that returns consistent cameras and geometry for all generated views at once. Gen3R (Huang et al., preprint, arXiv:2601.04090, January 2026) goes one step further. It adapts VGGT to produce geometry latents aligned with the appearance latents of a pretrained video diffusion model. The generator then outputs RGB video together with cameras, depth and point clouds. Generation and reconstruction become one model.

From video priors to 3D Gaussians in seconds

The “generate in 2D, reconstruct in 3D” lesson of section 3 was turned into fast, single-pass pipelines:

  • Stable Virtual Camera (Zhou et al., preprint, arXiv:2503.14489) is a generalist novel-view diffusion model. It takes any number of input views and target cameras and produces consistent views along long camera paths. It is the CAT3D idea as an open model.
  • GEN3C (Ren et al., CVPR 2025, arXiv:2503.03751) keeps a 3D cache: point clouds unprojected from predicted depth. It renders the cache along the requested camera path and uses the renders to condition video generation. This gives precise camera control and consistency, which is section 4’s loop with a video model inside.
  • Bolt3D (Szymanowicz et al., ICCV 2025, arXiv:2503.14445) is a latent diffusion model that directly generates Gaussian “splatter images” of a scene in seconds, with no per-scene optimization.
  • Lyra (Bahmani et al., preprint, arXiv:2509.19296) self-distills a camera-controlled video diffusion model into a decoder that outputs 3D Gaussians from the video latents. It therefore needs no multi-view training data, and the same trick extends to dynamic (4D) scenes. FlashWorld (Li et al., preprint, arXiv:2510.13678) generates 3D Gaussian scenes directly within seconds by distilling the quality of multi-view-oriented generation into a 3D-oriented generator.
  • FLAT (Kupyn et al., preprint, arXiv:2606.24876, June 2026) decodes triangle splats instead of Gaussians directly from video-diffusion latents in one pass. It reports better geometric accuracy, and a short refinement gives an opaque representation that game engines can use. This matters because opaque triangles plug into ordinary rasterizers (graphics post 2) and physics, unlike semi-transparent Gaussians.

Panorama-first world generators

A different 2025 recipe generates a 360° panorama first, because a panorama fixes the whole surround at once, and then lifts it to 3D:

  • HunyuanWorld 1.0 (HunyuanWorld Team, preprint, arXiv:2507.21809, July 2025) generates a panoramic proxy from text or an image and splits it into semantic layers (sky, background, objects). It lifts the layers to 3D and exports meshes, so worlds are explorable and editable in standard tools. HunyuanWorld-Voyager (Huang et al., preprint, arXiv:2506.04225) jointly generates RGB and depth video along a user camera path and keeps a world cache, which lets it extend scenes over long ranges with direct 3D reconstruction.
  • Matrix-3D (Yang et al., technical report, arXiv:2508.08086) generates panoramic video along a trajectory, then reconstructs 3D either with a fast feed-forward model or with optimization.
  • HY-World 2.0 (Team HY-World, preprint, arXiv:2604.14268, April 2026) unifies reconstruction, generation and simulation. It produces navigable 3DGS worlds from text or a single image through a four-stage pipeline, and it uses its feed-forward reconstruction model WorldMirror 2.0 for multi-view input. The authors describe it as an offline world model and report results comparable to Marble; that comparison is the authors’ own.

Marble becomes a platform, and Atlas

Section 6 described Marble’s public release (12 November 2025). Its 2026 trajectory is a good example of how a world generator turns into infrastructure. The sources are all World Labs posts:

  • The World API (21 January 2026) exposes world generation programmatically.
  • Spark 2.0 (14 April 2026) is World Labs’ web renderer for streaming large 3DGS worlds in the browser. Gaussian splats became a delivery format, not just a research representation (compare neural-rendering post 4).
  • Atlas (1 September 2026) is a new model, in early access for selected partners. It is described as a multimodal autoregressive diffusion transformer, pretrained from scratch on text, images, video and 3D. Each input image is anchored at a 3D pose in a shared spatial context. The model generates camera-controlled video (up to one minute at 1440p, according to the post), panoramas, and explicit point clouds or Gaussian splats. World Labs says Atlas will power future versions of Marble. Architecturally, Atlas is the hybrid of section 7: posed frames (as in RTFM, post 3) plus explicit 3D outputs (as in Marble).
  • On 28 September 2026 World Labs announced that it is joining AMD. World Labs also wrote about generating worlds for robot training (“Building Worlds That Train Robots”, July 2026), which links this post to simulation for embodied AI (graphics-engine post 5).

4D and objects

  • 4DNeX (Chen et al., preprint, arXiv:2508.13154) is a feed-forward image-to-4D model. It fine-tunes a video diffusion model to output, for each frame, RGB plus an XYZ point map, which is a “6D video” that converts directly into dynamic point clouds or Gaussians. Compare it with the per-scene SDS optimization of section 5.
  • Aether (Aether Team, preprint, arXiv:2503.18945) trains one geometry-aware model, on synthetic data, for three jobs: 4D reconstruction, action-conditioned video prediction and goal-conditioned visual planning. It shows that a 4D generator can double as a world model in the sense of post 1.
  • SAM 3D (SAM 3D Team, preprint, arXiv:2511.16624) reconstructs objects (shape, texture and layout) from a single natural image. Object-level 3D “for free” means a scene generator can populate worlds with separable, editable objects instead of one fused surface.

Explicit 3D as memory for interactive generation

The most interesting 2026 development joins this post with post 3. L3G-World (Choi et al., preprint, arXiv:2610.40614, October 2026) keeps a latent 3D Gaussian memory. A feed-forward updater adds new Gaussians only where the scene needs new geometry, and a memory-conditioned video diffusion transformer renders the next frames from it. The memory therefore grows with the explored scene, not with the length of the stream, as the toy figure shows. Compared with the most efficient prior 3D-memory baseline, the authors report up to 38.6x fewer 3D primitives and real-time generation at 9 fps.

The growth figure assumes how much of each frame is new. A small toy run measures it instead. A camera walks through a 24 m x 12 m room with five boxes (a 2D floor plan, 613 frames, 0.12 m per step). In each frame it casts 90 depth rays over a 90° field of view (range 7 m). It adds a surfel, one 25 cm piece of wall, to its memory only if that piece is not there yet. The walk explores the left part, repeats its first route exactly, and then explores the right part. The figure shows where the memory grew and how fast.

Three numbers stand out. While exploring, on average only about 3% of the surfels in a frame are new, because consecutive views overlap heavily. During the repeated route the new fraction is exactly zero and coverage stays flat at 42%: everything the camera sees can be rendered from memory, so a generator only has to render, not invent. At the end, storing every ray hit would give 43,011 primitives, while the deduplicated memory holds 475 surfels that cover 92% of the walls, about 90 times fewer. This is a noise-free 2D caricature (real depth is noisy and needs fusion rather than plain deduplication), but it shows why a memory that grows with $\lvert A\rvert$ is the right scaling.

What changed in section 7

  • Persistence vs. dynamics. Explicit memory is now used inside interactive video models (GEN3C, Voyager, L3G-World). The “memory” row of the trade-off table therefore no longer separates the two columns as sharply.
  • Speed. Single-image-to-3D-scene went from minutes (CAT3D) to seconds (Bolt3D, FlashWorld) with feed-forward decoders.
  • Engine-readiness. Output formats moved toward what engines consume: meshes (HunyuanWorld 1.0), triangle splats (FLAT) and streamable splats (Spark 2.0). For how splats and generated assets enter real engines, see graphics-engine post 6.
  • Still open. Generated worlds remain mostly static. Physically meaningful materials, articulated objects and agents are rare, and none of the systems above has a published evaluation of whether its worlds are useful for training agents (post 5’s update covers the first closed-loop benchmarks).

Exercises

  1. Derive the SDS gradient by differentiating $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{diff}}$ with the chain rule and identifying the term that is dropped. Under what assumption on the U-Net Jacobian would the dropped term vanish in expectation?
  2. In the SDS toy, add a classifier-free-guidance-like scale by multiplying eps_model(x_t, t) - eps by 5 and 20. How do the spread and mode coverage change? Relate this to the over-saturation of DreamFusion’s assets.
  3. Turn the toy into VSD: train a second small denoiser online on the current particles theta and use its prediction in place of eps. Does the spread approach that of the real data?
  4. In the depth-alignment script, replace the planar scene by one with a step discontinuity and a monocular depth that is a nonlinear function of the truth. Where does the global affine fit fail, and how would a local fit help?
  5. Take one row of the trade-off table in Section 7 and design a hybrid system that gets the best of both columns for that row. What would its training data be?
  6. (2025-2026 update) In the memory-growth toy, let the walk revisit a fraction $r$ of the time instead of one third, and let the fraction of new geometry per exploring frame be $f$. Write $N_{\text{frame}}(T)$ and $N_{\text{extent}}(T)$, and their ratio. For $f = 0.25$ and $r = 1/3$, what ratio do you get after 120 s, and how does it compare with the up-to-38.6x reduction that L3G-World reports against a 3D-memory baseline?
Hint

$N_{\text{frame}} = cT$ and $N_{\text{extent}} = c f (1 - r) T$, so the ratio is $1 / (f(1-r))$, which is $6$ for $f = 0.25$, $r = 1/3$. Larger reductions need a smaller $f$ (more overlap between frames, as in slow camera motion) or more revisiting. The baseline in the paper is a different, already-pruned 3D memory, so the numbers are not directly comparable.
