Imitation learning: behavior cloning, DAgger, ACT and Diffusion Policy

Why this matters

RL (post 2) needs a reward and a lot of trials, which is fine for walking in a GPU simulator and painful for folding a shirt or plugging in a cable. Imitation learning (IL) skips the reward: a human demonstrates the task, and the robot learns a policy that maps observations to the demonstrated actions. Since 2023, two ideas, action chunking and generative action models (diffusion and flow matching), made IL work for fine-grained, contact-rich manipulation from a few dozen demonstrations. They are also the action heads of today’s Vision-Language-Action models (post 5). This post builds IL from the ground up and ends with a working Diffusion Policy training loop. The simulators of the previous post are where you can practice it before touching a robot.

Prerequisites

  • Supervised learning with PyTorch; the RL notation of post 2 ($\pi$, trajectories, horizon $T$).
  • Diffusion models at the level of DDPM (forward noising, noise prediction). The world-models and neural-rendering tracks cover these too.
  • The action-space discussion of post 1 (joint vs. end-effector targets, impedance control).

1. Behavior cloning and covariate shift

Given demonstrations $\mathcal{D} = \lbrace(o_i, a_i)\rbrace$ from an expert policy $\pi^{\ast}$, behavior cloning (BC) fits a policy by maximum likelihood:

\[\theta^{\ast} = \arg\max_\theta \sum_{(o,a)\in\mathcal{D}} \log \pi_\theta(a \mid o).\]

With a fixed-variance Gaussian policy this is just MSE regression. BC is the oldest robot learning method (ALVINN drove a vehicle from camera images with a small neural network in 1988) and it is still the backbone of most manipulation systems. It has two classic failure modes.

Covariate shift and compounding errors

BC trains on states from the expert’s distribution $d_{\pi^{\ast}}$, but at test time the robot visits states from its own distribution $d_{\pi_\theta}$. A small mistake moves it to a state no demonstration covers, where it makes a bigger mistake, and so on. Ross and Bagnell (2010) formalized this: if the learned policy errs with probability $\epsilon$ on the expert’s states, the expected cost over a horizon $T$ can grow as $O(\epsilon T^2)$, while a learner trained on its own state distribution achieves $O(\epsilon T)$.

DAgger

DAgger (Dataset Aggregation; Ross, Gordon and Bagnell, AISTATS 2011) fixes the distribution mismatch by querying the expert on states the learner visits:

  1. Train $\pi_1$ on the expert demonstrations $\mathcal{D}$.
  2. For $i = 1, 2, \dots$: run $\pi_i$ (optionally mixed with the expert) to collect states; ask the expert to label them with $a = \pi^{\ast}(s)$; aggregate $\mathcal{D} \leftarrow \mathcal{D} \cup \lbrace(s, \pi^{\ast}(s))\rbrace$; retrain $\pi_{i+1}$.

DAgger has no-regret guarantees and the $O(\epsilon T)$ bound, but labelling arbitrary states is awkward for a human teleoperator. Practical variants let the human intervene only when the robot goes wrong (HG-DAgger, intervention data in many real systems), or use a privileged simulator policy as the expert, which is exactly the teacher-student distillation from post 2. Other remedies for covariate shift: inject noise during demonstrations so the data contains recoveries (DART), augment observations, and, most effectively in practice, predict action chunks (section 2).

Multimodality

Humans solve the same task in different ways: go around an obstacle on the left or on the right, grasp a mug by the handle or the rim. The MSE-optimal prediction is the average of the modes, which may be the one action that fails (straight into the obstacle). Remedies are expressive action distributions: discretized actions with a categorical output (RT-1, post 5), Gaussian mixtures (robomimic’s BC-RNN-GMM), energy-based models (Implicit BC), k-means “action bins” plus offsets (Behavior Transformers), VAEs (ACT), and diffusion or flow models (Diffusion Policy). The code at the end of this post shows MSE BC collapsing onto the obstacle while a diffusion policy keeps both modes.

2. Action chunking with transformers (ACT / ALOHA)

Zhao et al. (RSS 2023) built ALOHA, a low-cost bimanual teleoperation system in which a human moves two small “leader” arms and two “follower” arms mirror the joint positions, and ACT (Action Chunking with Transformers), which learned tasks like opening a translucent condiment cup and slotting a battery with 80 to 90% success from about 10 minutes of demonstrations. The key ideas:

  • Action chunking. Instead of one action per step, predict the next $k$ actions $a_{t:t+k}$ (e.g. $k = 100$ joint targets at 50 Hz). Chunking reduces the effective horizon by a factor of $k$, so compounding errors have fewer opportunities to accumulate, and it lets the model represent temporally correlated behaviour such as pauses, which single-step policies handle poorly.
  • Temporal ensembling. Querying the policy every step yields overlapping chunks. ACT averages all predictions for the current time step with exponential weights $w_i = \exp(-m\, i)$, where $i = 0$ is the oldest prediction, which smooths motion without lag.
  • CVAE training. A transformer encoder compresses the demonstrated chunk into a latent style variable $z$; the decoder (a transformer over ResNet image features from several cameras and the joint state) predicts the chunk given $z$. At test time $z$ is set to the prior mean. The objective is reconstruction (L1) plus a KL term.
  • Joint-space actions with a position controller, which avoids IK and singularities for the 6-DoF ALOHA arms.

Mobile ALOHA (Fu et al. CoRL 2024) put the system on a mobile base and showed that co-training with the static ALOHA data improves mobile manipulation. ALOHA Unleashed (Zhao et al. CoRL 2024) scaled data collection and used a diffusion-based policy for tasks like tying shoelaces and hanging shirts.

3. Diffusion Policy

Chi et al. (RSS 2023) model the conditional distribution of an action sequence with a denoising diffusion model. A policy step starts from Gaussian noise and iteratively denoises it into an action chunk, conditioned on recent observations. Across 12 tasks from 4 benchmarks it improved success rates by 46.9% on average over the prior state of the art.

Formulation

Let $A^0_t \in \mathbb{R}^{T_p\times d_a}$ be a chunk of $T_p$ future actions and $O_t$ the last $T_o$ observations. The DDPM forward process adds noise with a schedule $\bar\alpha_k$:

\[A^k_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_k}\,A^0_t + \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_k}\,\epsilon, \qquad \epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0, I),\]

and the network $\epsilon_\theta$ learns to predict that noise:

\[\mathcal{L}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{k,\,\epsilon,\,(O_t, A^0_t)\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\left\lVert \epsilon - \epsilon_\theta\!\left(A^k_t,\, k,\, O_t\right)\right\rVert^2\right].\]

At inference, starting from $A^K_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$, each reverse step is

\[A^{k-1}_t = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_k}}\left(A^k_t - \frac{\beta_k}{\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_k}}\,\epsilon_\theta(A^k_t, k, O_t)\right) + \sigma_k z, \qquad z\sim\mathcal{N}(0, I),\]

with $\beta_k = 1-\alpha_k$ and $\sigma_k^2 = \beta_k$. Since $\epsilon_\theta$ is (up to scale) the score $\nabla_A \log p(A \mid O)$, sampling is noisy gradient ascent on the log-density of actions, which naturally represents multiple modes and high-dimensional, temporally coherent action sequences.

Design choices that matter

  • Receding horizon. Predict $T_p$ steps (e.g. 16), execute only the first $T_a$ (e.g. 8), then replan from new observations ($T_o = 2$ frames). Executing a chunk gives temporal consistency; replanning gives reactivity.
  • Conditioning, not joint modelling. Observations condition the denoiser (via FiLM layers in a 1-D temporal CNN U-Net, or cross-attention in a transformer variant) instead of being denoised, so the vision encoder runs once per policy step, not once per denoising step.
  • Position control in end-effector space worked better than velocity control in their experiments: absolute targets suffer less from compounding drift.
  • Fast sampling. Training uses 100 DDPM steps; inference can use DDIM with about 10 steps. Later work replaces diffusion with flow matching (pi0, post 5) or consistency distillation to reach real-time rates.

Extensions include 3D Diffusion Policy (Ze et al. RSS 2024), which conditions on point clouds, and the extended journal version (Chi et al. IJRR 2024).

4. Collecting demonstrations

The data is the real bottleneck. Common interfaces:

Interface Examples Pros Cons
Kinesthetic teaching pushing a compliant arm by hand intuitive, no extra hardware human in the camera view, single arm
Leader-follower arms ALOHA, GELLO low latency, bimanual, joint-space, force feel via backdrivability robot-specific hardware
VR / AR teleoperation Quest or Vision Pro hand tracking, Open-TeleVision immersive, dexterous hands, remote retargeting, latency, no haptics (post 6)
Space mouse / phone robomimic, RoboTurk cheap, scalable slow, hard for dexterous tasks
Hand-held grippers UMI no robot needed, in-the-wild data needs pose tracking and embodiment matching
Human video Ego4D, EgoMimic huge scale no actions, embodiment gap (post 6)

UMI (Universal Manipulation Interface; Chi et al. RSS 2024) is a hand-held, 3D-printed gripper with a wrist-mounted GoPro. A fisheye lens and side mirrors give a wide, implicitly stereo view; visual-inertial SLAM recovers the gripper’s 6-DoF trajectory; and the policy interface accounts for latency differences between data collection and deployment. Because the observation (wrist camera) and action (end-effector pose, gripper width) are the same on the hand-held device and on the robot, data collected in kitchens and parks transfers to real arms. The same principle, make the data interface and the robot interface identical, recurs throughout embodied AI.

Practical tips that matter more than the architecture: consistent demonstrators, calibrated cameras, synchronised timestamps, recording the controller’s actual targets (not the measured state), and collecting recovery behaviour on purpose.

5. Large datasets and cross-embodiment learning

Single-lab datasets have hundreds to tens of thousands of episodes. Pooling data across labs and robots is the robotics version of web-scale pre-training:

  • Open X-Embodiment (ICRA 2024): a collaboration of 21 institutions that standardized datasets from 22 robots, covering 527 skills (160,266 tasks), into the RLDS format. RT-1-X and RT-2-X trained on the mixture outperformed single-robot policies; on the authors’ evaluation RT-2-X showed about three times the success of RT-2 on emergent skills.
  • BridgeData V2 (CoRL 2023): tabletop manipulation with a low-cost WidowX arm across many environments; a common pre-training and evaluation set.
  • DROID (RSS 2024): 76k teleoperated trajectories (about 350 hours) on Franka arms in 564 scenes, collected by many institutions to maximize scene diversity.
  • LeRobot (Hugging Face): a PyTorch library and data format with implementations of ACT, Diffusion Policy and VLA models (including SmolVLA and pi0), plus a hub of community datasets recorded on low-cost arms.

Cross-embodiment training raises its own questions: how to align action spaces (end-effector deltas vs. joints), how to handle different camera setups, and how to weight datasets. Post 5 discusses how VLA models answer them.

6. A runnable Diffusion Policy training step

The script below implements the core of Diffusion Policy on a toy problem that exposes multimodality: demonstrations start near the origin and move forward while bending either left or right around an obstacle. It trains a noise-prediction network over 8-step action chunks with a cosine DDPM schedule and, for comparison, an MSE behavior cloning model. The denoiser is a small MLP (the paper uses a 1-D temporal U-Net or a transformer), but the training step and the sampler are the real thing. It runs in about half a minute on a CPU.

import math
import torch
import torch.nn as nn
import torch.nn.functional as F

torch.manual_seed(0)
H, A, O = 8, 2, 2          # action horizon (chunk length), action dim, observation dim
K = 100                    # diffusion steps

# ---- toy demonstrations: from the start, go around an obstacle either left OR right ----
def make_demos(n):
    obs = torch.randn(n, O) * 0.05                       # start position near the origin
    side = torch.randint(0, 2, (n, 1)).float() * 2 - 1   # -1 = left, +1 = right (multimodal!)
    t = torch.linspace(0, 1, H)
    x = t.expand(n, H)                                   # move forward in x
    y = side * torch.sin(math.pi * t) * 0.5              # bend left or right in y
    act = torch.stack([x, y], -1) + obs[:, None, :]      # [n, H, 2] chunk of future waypoints
    return obs, act

# ---- noise schedule (DDPM, cosine) ----
s = 0.008
f = torch.cos((torch.arange(K + 1) / K + s) / (1 + s) * math.pi / 2) ** 2
alpha_bar = (f / f[0])[1:]                               # \bar{alpha}_k, k = 1..K
alphas = alpha_bar / torch.cat([torch.ones(1), alpha_bar[:-1]])
betas = (1 - alphas).clamp(max=0.999)

class NoisePredictor(nn.Module):
    """eps_theta(a_k, k, o): an MLP over the flattened action chunk (a 1-D U-Net in the paper)."""
    def __init__(self, hidden=256):
        super().__init__()
        self.net = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(H * A + O + 32, hidden), nn.Mish(),
                                 nn.Linear(hidden, hidden), nn.Mish(),
                                 nn.Linear(hidden, H * A))
    def forward(self, a_noisy, k, obs):
        freqs = torch.exp(torch.arange(16) * (-math.log(1000.0) / 16))
        emb = k[:, None].float() * freqs[None]                     # sinusoidal step embedding
        emb = torch.cat([emb.sin(), emb.cos()], -1)
        x = torch.cat([a_noisy.flatten(1), obs, emb], -1)
        return self.net(x).view(-1, H, A)

def train_step(model, opt, obs, act):
    """One Diffusion Policy training step: predict the noise added to an action chunk."""
    B = act.shape[0]
    k = torch.randint(0, K, (B,))                                   # random diffusion step
    eps = torch.randn_like(act)
    ab = alpha_bar[k].view(B, 1, 1)
    a_noisy = ab.sqrt() * act + (1 - ab).sqrt() * eps               # forward process q(a_k | a_0)
    loss = F.mse_loss(model(a_noisy, k, obs), eps)
    opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); opt.step()
    return loss.item()

@torch.no_grad()
def sample(model, obs):
    """DDPM reverse process: start from Gaussian noise, denoise K times."""
    a = torch.randn(obs.shape[0], H, A)
    for k in reversed(range(K)):
        kk = torch.full((obs.shape[0],), k)
        eps = model(a, kk, obs)
        a = (a - betas[k] / (1 - alpha_bar[k]).sqrt() * eps) / alphas[k].sqrt()
        if k > 0:
            a = a + betas[k].sqrt() * torch.randn_like(a)
    return a

if __name__ == "__main__":
    model = NoisePredictor()
    opt = torch.optim.AdamW(model.parameters(), lr=1e-3, weight_decay=1e-6)
    bc = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(O, 256), nn.Mish(), nn.Linear(256, H * A))   # MSE baseline
    opt_bc = torch.optim.Adam(bc.parameters(), lr=1e-3)
    for it in range(3000):
        obs, act = make_demos(256)
        loss = train_step(model, opt, obs, act)
        l_bc = F.mse_loss(bc(obs).view(-1, H, A), act)
        opt_bc.zero_grad(); l_bc.backward(); opt_bc.step()
        if it % 1000 == 0:
            print(f"it {it}: diffusion loss {loss:.4f}, BC loss {l_bc.item():.4f}")
    obs = torch.zeros(1000, O)
    mid_dp = sample(model, obs)[:, H // 2, 1]           # lateral offset in the middle of the chunk
    with torch.no_grad():
        mid_bc = bc(obs).view(-1, H, A)[:, H // 2, 1]
    print("diffusion: fraction left %.2f, right %.2f, mean |y| %.3f" %
          ((mid_dp < -0.25).float().mean(), (mid_dp > 0.25).float().mean(), mid_dp.abs().mean()))
    print("MSE BC:    mean y %.3f (collapses to the average, i.e. into the obstacle)" % mid_bc.mean())

Output:

it 0: diffusion loss 1.0366, BC loss 0.2291
it 1000: diffusion loss 0.0401, BC loss 0.0542
it 2000: diffusion loss 0.0299, BC loss 0.0547
diffusion: fraction left 0.56, right 0.43, mean |y| 0.484
MSE BC:    mean y 0.022 (collapses to the average, i.e. into the obstacle)

The diffusion policy samples both modes in roughly the demonstrated 50/50 proportion, with a lateral offset close to the true $0.5 \sin(\pi \cdot 4/7) \approx 0.49$ at the middle of the chunk, while MSE BC predicts $y \approx 0$: straight through the obstacle. Its loss also plateaus near 0.055, about the variance of the two modes it averages. To turn this into a real policy: replace obs with image features from a ResNet or ViT (trained end-to-end with random-crop augmentation), use a temporal U-Net, normalize actions to $[-1, 1]$, keep an exponential moving average of the weights for evaluation, and execute the first $T_a$ actions of each sampled chunk in a receding-horizon loop. LeRobot’s implementation is a good reference.

7. Beyond plain imitation

  • Inverse RL and adversarial imitation. Recover a reward that explains the demonstrations, then optimize it. GAIL (Ho and Ermon 2016) trains a discriminator between expert and policy state-action pairs and uses it as a reward; adversarial motion priors in character animation follow the same idea.
  • Offline RL uses rewards on fixed datasets to improve beyond the demonstrator.
  • Learning from play and language. Unstructured teleoperation “play” data relabeled with goals or language instructions (CALVIN) feeds language-conditioned policies, the step from IL to VLA models.

Exercises

  1. Reproduce the compounding-error effect: train MSE BC on noise-free demonstrations of a 1-D point tracking a sinusoid, roll it out with small observation noise, and plot error versus horizon. Then add DAgger with a scripted expert.
  2. In the diffusion script, replace the MLP with a 1-D convolutional U-Net with FiLM conditioning and compare sample quality for $H = 16$.
  3. Implement DDIM sampling with 10 steps and measure how the left/right fractions and the trajectory quality change.
  4. Replace the diffusion objective by flow matching: sample $t\sim U(0,1)$, set $A^t = (1-t)\epsilon + t A^0$ and regress the velocity $A^0 - \epsilon$; sample by integrating the ODE with 10 Euler steps.
  5. Implement ACT-style temporal ensembling on top of the diffusion sampler (query every step, average overlapping predictions with exponential weights) and compare smoothness with receding-horizon execution.
